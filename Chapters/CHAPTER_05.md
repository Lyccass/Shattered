# Chapter 5 - People

For a second after stepping through the arch, Adrian lost track of everything. There was no floor beneath him, no weight in his limbs, and no stable sense that his body still existed as anything more than an interrupted thought. Darkness pressed in from every side so completely that even his own mind felt briefly distant.

Then the world came back all at once.

Wind hit him first. Open sky followed. He stood on broken stone at the edge of a ruin that might once have been part of the Helix facility, though whatever it had been in his time had long since collapsed into the land. Grass rolled out across low hills ahead of him, small clusters of trees broke the open ground at uneven intervals, and mountains stood far off against the horizon. There was no one anywhere in sight.

Adrian turned at once and looked back at the arch.

There was no chamber behind it now, no dark corridor, no trace of the place he had stepped out of. The structure stood alone on the broken stone like an ordinary arch set down in the middle of open country, which would have been almost reassuring if he had not just come through it.

*So that means there were not only monsters, stats, and magic, but teleportation as well. This is crazy.* Fine. Whether any of it made sense was well beyond him at the moment, and worrying at it now would not help.

"I am probably dead," he muttered. "Or hallucinating. That would honestly be easier."

Neither possibility helped. The ground around him was too open, the arch explained nothing, and staying beside the thing that had just thrown him across gods-knew-how-much distance felt like a very good way to make his next mistake early. He adjusted his grip on the spear, picked the nearest rise with enough height to show him more of the land, and started walking.

The walk took longer than Adrian had expected. Judging distance across open ground was evidently not one of his strengths. *If something out here wants me dead, I am making that very easy for it.*

He had spent most of his life in and around cities, and even when work dragged him out into the field, there had usually been roads, fences, farm buildings, pylons, or some other human measure to break the land into something readable. Out here there was only grass, stone, wind, a few trees and far too much space.

The effort started biting not long after he left the arch behind. His illness might have been gone, but that did not make him superhuman. His ribs still pulled when he breathed too deeply, his knee had not forgiven the earlier fight, and the simple act of hauling himself uphill with a spear and a half-understood body proved quickly enough that his new stats had not turned him into some untouchable fantasy hero. They had just made him more capable than he had been.

He stopped once to catch his breath, drank sparingly some of the water ressources he had found in the place he had waken up in, then used the spear as a walking stick and kept climbing towards the nearest rise.

By the time he reached it, the sun had already begun to sink. Adrian turned and looked back the way he had come.

The arch still stood in the distance on its patch of broken stone, small now against the sweep of the land. There was no visible portal, no darkness in its centre, no sign of the place he had stepped through beyond the fact that the structure remained. Whatever had brought him here was gone for the moment, and staring at it from farther away only made one thing clear: going back was not a plan.

Looking down the far side, Adrian saw the first break in the land that had nothing to do with wind, stone, or the slow untidy spread of grass. A narrow line cut across the slope below, faint from where he stood but too deliberate to be natural once he fixed on it. The grass along its centre had been bent lower than the rest, and in a few places worn back to pale soil. Near one turn in the line stood a post of dark worked stone about waist-high, half-buried and set at a slight angle, one side flattened smooth in a way no weather had any business producing.

He stayed where he was for a few seconds and watched it as though the post might get up and explain itself. Nothing moved around it. The line through the grass held steady. No birds rose. No shapes broke from cover. After another slow look across the surrounding ground, he started down towards it.

The descent went faster than the climb had, though that mostly meant he had more energy left over to notice every time his knee complained and every time the pull in his ribs tightened when he planted badly. The wind had a cleaner edge lower on the slope, stronger too, pressing the grass flat in long shifting bands that made the whole hillside look as if something enormous were breathing under it. Adrian kept the spear in both hands now rather than using it as a walking stick. The ground felt too open for one-handed optimism.

Up close, the path looked even less accidental. Too many repeated footfalls had gone this way for too long. The line was not paved, fenced, or built up in any serious sense, but people had used it often enough to teach the land a route. *People use this route.* Realising that he was not the only sapient beeing around, a tension he didnt even know he held left his body in an instant.

The stone had been shaped with tools rather than split out of the earth by chance, and shallow grooves covered its smoothed face in neat deliberate rows.

Writing.

Adrian crouched in front of it and looked harder, because there was always a stupid part of the brain that wanted the answer to be simple even after the universe had spent the better part of a day proving simplicity was dead. The characters meant nothing to him. They were not English worn strange by time, not a symbol system he could bluff his way through by guessing arrows and warnings, and not close enough to any alphabet he knew to even fake the comfort of resemblance. Whoever had cut them had known exactly what they were doing. The strokes were measured, their spacing regular, and the shallower marks at the lower edge looked newer than the rest, as if somebody had refreshed part of the inscription after the stone had already stood here a long while.

At the base of the post he found older proof. Three stones had been set in a loose ring and blackened by fire. Ash still sat protected in the centre, damp only in the top layer. A broken strip of leather lay half under one of the stones. A little farther off, where the grass thinned over compacted soil, boot prints had dried hard in the ground and survived long enough to keep their shape.

People, then. Current people, not just the cold remains of a dead facility and its dead worker. 

He straightened slowly and looked from the writing to the path and then out across the open country beyond it. Finding people was one problem solved. It also created several new ones all at once. There was no reason to assume they spoke English. No reason to assume they could read anything he wrote, if he even had a way to write it. No reason to assume they would see a lone stranger carrying a spear and dressed in a coat stolen from a corpse and think anything charitable at all.

"Brilliant," he muttered, because the silence had started closing in again. "Civilisation. Immediately complicated."

The path kept going downslope and curved round a shallow fold in the land where the grass grew taller. Adrian followed it because there was no practical alternative. The light had already begun to lower, and the difference between spending the night near some form of human route and spending it alone in a field with no idea what hunted after dark was not hard to make.

The ground gave him a little more as he went, though not as much as he would have liked. The soil had softened near a run of stones, and there were enough prints pressed into it to tell him more than one person had come this way recently. Beyond that, he was guessing. Some looked smaller than the others. One print seemed deeper at the heel, which might have meant more weight or simply a heavier step. Another did not look quite like a boot at all, though he had no idea whether that meant local fashion, local practicality, or something stranger.

He crouched for a moment and looked anyway, because uncertain information still beat none. What he could say with confidence was simple enough: nobody had been running, nobody had churned the ground into a panic, and the path had been used by people who knew where they were going well enough not to hesitate every few steps. That was already more useful than the rest.

That did not stop him from almost missing them.

The land opened again past the fold, and Adrian had just enough warning to drop into the grass before the movement ahead resolved into people rather than wind. He went down hard on one knee, swore under his breath when the joint protested, and then stayed still with the spear tucked close while he looked through the stems.

They were farther off than he had first thought, spread across the lower slope in a loose line that kept changing shape as they moved. Six of them, maybe seven if the last flicker behind the others had been a person rather than brush. Armed. Human-shaped, at least, which was the first thing that mattered.

He took his time with the second.

They did not move like soldiers on parade. No matched step, no polished formation, no effort spent looking impressive. Their spacing was practical instead, wide enough that one problem would not catch all of them at once and tight enough that nobody drifted out of help. Every so often one stopped, looked off to one side, then moved again when the others adjusted around them. They were not strolling. They were crossing dangerous ground with the sort of routine attention that suggested dangerous ground was normal.

The clothes did not help him place them. If anything, they made the whole thing stranger. One wore a plain knee-length tunic belted over dark trousers with a stitched leather vest on top and mismatched bracers at the wrists. Another had a robe or coat that fell below the knee, though the hem had been cut back and mended so often it had become its own shape, with a short mail shirt visible underneath when the wind caught it open. A third carried a spear and round shield while wearing what looked like quilted layers under one shoulder guard of worked metal and no matching piece on the other side. Nothing matched cleanly. The group looked less like a unit issued from one authority than the practical result of people dressing from whatever a rough frontier allowed them to keep.

The weapons told him a little more. Not much, and certainly not enough to pretend expertise, but enough. They were a mixed, practical mess rather than a matched set, and nobody wore them like decoration. Nothing swung loose. Nobody kept fiddling with straps or grips. The gear sat on them as if it belonged there. That did not tell Adrian who they were,bu it did tell him the weapons were real. *And if my luck keeps up, probably against me* He noted with an sour expression

He followed parallel to them for a while because approaching strangers in open country without first learning anything useful about them sounded like a poor use of the body he had only just got back. The stalking part of it felt ridiculous, but so did almost everything else that day, and at least this kind of ridiculous had a method. He stayed above them when he could, using folds in the land, thicker grass, and the occasional stand of scrub to keep from being outlined against the sky. Twice he had to flatten himself fully because one of them stopped and looked upslope.

The second time, Adrian was sure he had been seen.

The man had turned without warning and simply stood there, one hand low near his spear, looking straight towards the patch of grass that now contained Adrian and most of his immediate dignity. The distance was long enough that facial details were impossible, but posture carried well out here. Alert, still, waiting. Adrian slowed his breathing, pressed himself lower, and hated how loudly his own pulse seemed to travel through his body. The grass moved. The wind shifted. After several seconds that felt much longer, the man glanced towards the others, said something, and started forward again.

Adrian let the breath out slowly through his nose and stayed where he was until the group had moved another twenty yards.

"That was close enough," he muttered.

He trailed them a little lower after that and eventually got what he had been straining for from the beginning: a proper look at faces.

Human.

Not approximately. Not if he squinted and pretended. Human in all the obvious ways that mattered to a man who had spent the last several hours revising his idea of what counted as possible. One woman turned her head while speaking to the figure beside her and gave him a clean profile against the light. Human nose. Human mouth. Human brow. Another pushed hair back from his face with the heel of one hand and revealed a jaw that would not have looked strange on a train platform or in a bad queue at a Tesco. Adrian had not realised quite how tightly some part of him had been wound around that question until it loosened.

"Thank fuck," he said very softly.

The relief lasted right up until he heard them.

The voices drifted upslope in pieces when the wind turned, low at first and then clearer as the group slowed near another bend in the path. Human voices, human cadence, real conversation moving back and forth with the ordinary ease of people who expected to be understood. Adrian caught none of it. Not one word. The sounds were not slurred, not distorted by distance into nonsense, and not hidden under any magical strangeness he could blame for the failure. It was language. It simply was not his.

He stayed crouched in the grass and listened harder, as though effort might bully meaning out of it. One speaker had a rougher voice than the others. Another clipped the ends of words short. A third spoke more slowly and got interrupted halfway through by somebody behind him. None of that helped. He might as well have been listening to birds argue.

*Human is good. Human who cannot understand me is less good.*

They were human. That mattered. It mattered a great deal. They also lived in a world where he could not understand the first thing they said, and if he walked down there now with a stranger's clothes, a spear in hand, and no language in common, the outcome of that meeting was no longer something he could safely guess. *Or maybe I can guess it, but I don`t want to. Because the I have the feeling it will not be in favour for me.*

Adrian watched them a little longer from the slope while the sun sank lower and the light went warmer across the grass. Alone was bad. Approaching blind might be worse. The trouble was that by the look of the country around him, dark would soon begin making both options uglier.

He tightened his grip on the spear and kept his eyes on the group, already aware that whatever he did next was going to be a gamble.



From the higher ridge, Keiran Vale noticed the planes stir a moment before it opened.

The disturbance travelled through the old line beneath the hill first, a brief tightening in the land, then a pulse through the worked stone below. A heartbeat later the arch gave way and deposited a single human onto the broken platform at its base.

A lone human emerging from a failing route was unusual enough to justify a little patience.  No local marks worth noticing, dead-Earth salvage worn with no understanding of how wrong it looked, fresh injuries, and a spear carried like a walking stick rather than a chosen discipline. The man had the expression of someone trying very hard to remain functional while reality became progressively less cooperative.

The human checked the arch first, then the horizon, then the nearest rise. Sensible. Not because the land was kind, but because higher ground at least offered information, and information was the only useful thing available to him in any quantity.

Fear sat on him plainly enough, though it had not yet made him stupid. That was worth something. Plenty of stronger people performed worse when dropped into uncertainty with better odds and more help.

Keiran watched him climb. He watched him find the marker post and linger over the inscription long enough to confirm the obvious conclusion. No language, then. No local guidance. No escort. The route had not simply pushed out another March fool a little farther from home than intended. It had delivered someone genuinely separate.

By the time the human noticed the frontier band below, Keiran's attention had sharpened from idle to deliberate. The man hesitated, assessed, and chose caution over panic.. Running straight at armed strangers would have been a studipid decision.

Keiran remained where he was and let the scene continue without him. "What a curious little fella", he mused mildly curious now, as the lone human began shadowing the group through the grass.



Adrian stayed where he was, crouched low in the grass with the spear held close and his weight resting more on his good leg than the other one. The group below had slowed near a line of darker stone half-buried in the slope, and from this distance he could finally make out a little more than movement, shape, and steel. As far as he could tell from his vantage point, they were all human.

It already was stupid, admittedly. He was crouched in waist-high grass on an unfamiliar hillside in some impossible future or virtual reality with a spear stolen from a dead man's cache, trying to judge whether the first living people he had seen since waking up were more dangerous than spending the night alone. The answer might easily have been yes. The trouble was that alone still felt worse.

He shifted to ease the pull in his ribs and began edging along the slope, meaning to keep them in sight a little longer before he committed. Closer first. Learn what he could. Then decide.

Then the wind shifted, and with it came a noise from behind him.

Something crashed through the grass, charging right at him.

Adrian started to turn and only got halfway there before it hit him low and hard enough to take his footing out from under him. He went down on his side with a muffled curse and the spear trapped awkwardly between his body and the slope. For one bright, furious second all he saw and felt was bristled hide, hot rank breath, and the white shock of pain where a tusk glanced off his bandaged ribs and made everything in his body light up.

"What is this thing?" Adrian cursed under his breath. It looked like a boar, but meaner, with dark scales plating much of its body and coarse bristles running along its neck. It looked like an armoured living tank.

It had the heavy front and brutal low charge of one, with coarse dark bristles running along a neck too thick for grace and a pair of tusks that curved higher than any normal pig had business growing. Its shoulders came nearly to his hip even with the angle of the slope against it, and its small eyes had that flat animal certainty that meant it had already decided what he was for.

"Fuck off," Adrian snapped, which naturally failed to impress it.

The beast came again before he had fully untangled himself. He dragged the spear free, rolled onto one knee, and got the shaft across in time to keep the next charge from turning his stomach into mush. The impact still drove him back through the grass. The butt of the spear skidded over stone. His right knee folded half a second too far under the force, and for a horrible instant the whole exchange threatened to become his last, brief act of defiance before he was trampled to death.

The beast shoved against the shaft, head twisting, tusks scraping wood with a noise that made Adrian's shoulders try to lock. He let it push once, gave ground instead of fighting stupid leverage with tired arms, and then snapped the point down and forward as the beast's weight shifted.

The spearhead punched in high behind the shoulder, not as deep as he wanted but deep enough to matter. The animal screamed and thrashed sideways, nearly wrenching the weapon out of his hands. Adrian held on with all his might, planted one boot, and tried to drag the point free for a second thrust.

He was too slow.

The beast tore loose first and came round again with blood darkening the bristles along one side. Its charge was shorter this time, less certain, but the slope let it accelerate far faster than it should have. In desperation Adrian threw his empty hand out to brace against it as it came in. It did not stop the attack. It slammed into him anyway, all compact weight and fury, and they both went down in a sliding mess of grass, stone, and mud.

The impact rattled him and tore the spear from his grip, sending it tumbling away down the slope.

The tusks missed the softest parts of him by luck, angle, or whatever small amount of goodwill the universe still had left to spend, and one raked instead across the heavy coat at his hip. Adrian got a forearm against the beast's neck, found the knife by feel, and drew it with no elegance at all. The thing squealed and snapped its head sideways, trying to get a better angle at him. He drove the blade in under the jaw.

The knife broke.
