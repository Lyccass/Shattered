# Chapter 1 - Cold times ahead

By the time Adrian reached the service stairs, his left leg had started lying to him again. It never failed in a clean, useful way that could be planned around. Instead it inserted a cheap little delay between intention and movement, just enough to make ordinary things feel negotiated. Stairs, kerbs, wet floors, door thresholds, any surface that used to be beneath notice suddenly expected terms.

The stairwell smelled of wet concrete, dust, and old paint. Someone had left a coil of orange cable across the first landing, not enough to block the way so much as to turn the descent into a mild act of sabotage. Adrian stopped at the top, shifted the canvas field bag on his shoulder, and took the rail before he moved. Two years earlier he would have been halfway down before the cable had even registered. Now he watched his foot placement like an old man in a public safety advert, and that still annoyed him more than the pain itself.

He took the stairs carefully because the alternative was falling over in front of a cleaner and then pretending that had been part of the plan. At the bottom he leaned a shoulder against the wall, pulled out his phone, and reread the message he already knew by heart.

`Appointment confirmed. Intake window: 14:00-14:30.`

Helix did polished language well. Everything they sent him sounded calm, humane, and aggressively reasonable, which was impressive for a company selling glorified freezer storage to dying people with money and bad odds. Cryogenic preservation became future-directed care, and a desperate gamble became a continuity pathway. Adrian had read the brochure often enough to remember the important part without opening it again. There was no guarantee of recovery, revival, legal continuity, memory integrity, bodily condition, or even personal identity under any currently recognised medical framework. If this worked, medicine in some distant future might fix what the present could not. If it did not, Helix would still have been paid before it became his problem.

He slid the phone back into his pocket and pushed through the stairwell door into the brittle afternoon. The service car park behind the district rail office was half full, with two vans, one hatchback, and a delivery lorry idling by the kerb. Beyond the chain-link fence, the overground line cut past the back of the building in dull steel, graffiti, and damp winter light. A maintenance unit crawled along the far track, and his attention went where it always went first. He noticed the patch repairs on the retaining wall before anything else — one section newer than the rest, already weathering at a different rate. The third fence post from the gate had a lean in it too.

Now his mother was waiting in the car with the engine running because getting in and out cost less if the heat was already on. He opened the passenger door and lowered himself into the seat with more care than he would have liked. The car smelled of old fabric, coffee, and the hand cream she had used for so long it might as well have been part of his genetic code by now. The heater was, naturally, up too high.

"You forgot your scarf," she said.

Adrian looked down at the scarf sitting in his lap. "Impressive catch."

"You brought it. You forgot to wear it."

"That feels like accounting fraud."

She ignored that, reached across, and straightened it against his coat — the same quick, competent motion she had used when he was a kid and already late for school. He let her do it. There were only so many bits of dignity left worth defending, and scarf management did not make the list.

"It's cold," she said.

"It's six degrees."

"Yes. Cold."

"You grew up by the water. Your standards should be better."

The corner of her mouth twitched, which was probably as good as he was getting. She pulled out of the car park and into traffic.

Mid-afternoon in the city moved with its usual irritated drag. Vans crowded the bus lane, pedestrians crossed where they clearly should not have, and a delivery cyclist threaded between them with the kind of confidence that suggested both deadlines and a death wish. Adrian watched the shopfronts slide by and tried not to turn any of it into something grand. Sometimes the last look at your old life was a kebab shop you had meant to try for eight years, discount posters in a chemist window, and a shut-down laundrette with a crooked sign.

At a red light his mother adjusted her grip on the wheel, and his gaze drifted automatically towards a hairline crack in the signal box on the corner, a fresh paving patch where utility works had been done badly and cheaply, and one tram line sagging slightly more than the others near the crossing. He was still doing it. He wondered if that habit would survive him better than the rest, whether some version of him, if there ever was one, would still look at the world and start sorting weak points from useful ones.

His mother drummed her thumb once against the wheel. "You don't need to joke with them."

"I wasn't planning to."

"Good."

"I save my best material for family."

She looked at him the way she had been looking at him all year. "Adrian."

He let it go because he knew what she meant. No turning himself into the pleasant, manageable sort of dying man private facilities liked best. No rewarding anybody for saying continuity pathway with a straight face.

The buildings flattened as they left the city centre, giving way to offices, warehouses, and a fenced solar field before the Helix Continuity Institute finally appeared beyond a broad turn-off lined with winter-pale shrubs and tasteful signs trying very hard not to resemble directions to a private mausoleum.

`Helix Continuity Institute`
`Extended Preservation and Future Recovery Services`

The building itself was low, white, expensive, and somehow evasive. It had too much glass, too many softened edges, and the sort of architecture meant to reassure wealthy people that medicine was not really medicine if enough money got involved. Adrian did not buy it. The public entrance was all calm restraint, while the loading bay round the side was much larger than it needed to be. Emergency power housings sat behind decorative screening. The west wall had already had its lower panels replaced once.

"If anyone says continuity pathway in person," Adrian said, "I'm leaving."

"No, you're not."

"You can't know that."

"I really can."

That got him. "Fair."

She parked near the entrance, but neither of them moved straight away. Rain had started again, too light to hear properly and too steady to ignore. It tracked across the windscreen in thin clear lines and blurred the building lights into something softer than they had any right to be. His mother kept both hands on the wheel.

"I brought the folder," she said.

"I know."

"Your ID is in the front pocket."

Rain moved across the windscreen in the pause.

"I know."

"And the-"

"Mum."

She stopped. That was what she had picked, then: pocket placement, document order, practical details small enough to win against. He looked at her profile for a moment, at the set jaw, the dry eyes. When he was fourteen and had broken his wrist falling off a retaining wall he definitely should not have been climbing, she had spent three hours in A&E explaining to increasingly tired nurses exactly how long they had kept them waiting. When the diagnosis came years later, she had done the same thing to consultants in nicer rooms with worse chairs. Worry always came out of her sideways; she had spent most of his life getting better at the logistics.

"I'm not doing this because I believe in it," he said.

She nodded once. "I know."

"I know how it sounds."

"I know."

"It's a bad bet."

That made her turn to him. "It's your bet."

The disease had been taking choice off him in layers, first long drives, then ladders, then the kind of days that had once just been Tuesday. Every month left a little less of his own life within easy reach.

It was probably a cleaner, more expensive way to die, wrapped in legal language and filtered air. Or a scam with better branding than most — he genuinely could not tell, and the honest answer was that it did not matter. It was still a door, however stupid, and he was not ready to sit still while the walls kept moving inward.

"Right," he said at last, reaching for the door handle.

His hand slipped, barely at all, but enough. His mother did not look at his hand. She just leaned over, pressed the handle from the inside, and pushed the door open against the rain before he had to fail a second time. Neither of them mentioned it. He got out.

Inside, the entrance floor had expansion joints cut wider than this building needed. Adrian filed it away because apparently his brain intended to keep doing that until the sedatives won. The lobby was warm, white, and so committed to calm that it came off hostile. Soft instrumental music seeped out of somewhere invisible. The reception desk curved in a gentle, human-centred arc, and plants stood in matte ceramic pots like they had all signed consent forms.

A man in a charcoal suit detached himself from the desk with the smoothness of someone who had spent long practice on the right approach speed. "Mr Keller?"

Adrian nearly laughed. "That's never encouraging."

The man's smile shifted by about two millimetres, enough to acknowledge humour and not enough to invite more of it. "We're ready for you whenever you are."

He and his mother were taken through a side door into a consultation room that looked like it had been designed by somebody who thought grief could be managed with beige upholstery and filtered water. A screen was already awake on the table. Three printed forms sat beside it in a tidy stack. Of course they did.

"If you would just confirm identity and final consent," the man said.

Adrian sat. His mother stayed standing.

The first page covered identity, medical history, and acknowledgement that the service was experimental. The second confirmed that preservation, once initiated, could not be reversed by any currently available method. The third was mostly Helix explaining all the interesting ways it was not liable if the future turned out to be uncooperative.

He read all of it. About halfway through the second form, a technician in pale scrubs came in with a tray of monitors and adhesive pads. He was in his thirties, maybe, with tired eyes and the careful patience of somebody who explained the same bad odds for a living.

"These are standard," he said when Adrian kept reading.

"I assumed so."

"We can answer any questions you have."

"Good."

His mother set the folder on the chair beside him and folded her arms. The technician waited. Adrian finished the page anyway, signed the first form, then passed it back.

The next ten minutes were mostly organised handling. A wrist tag. A blood-pressure cuff. Chest pads that felt too cold going on. The technician explained each step in a calm, low voice that made everything sound straightforward enough to do on a lunch break. Adrian let him get on with it until a physician came in to do the formal confirmation.

She had silver-framed glasses, perfect posture, and the sort of composed face that suggested either long practice or advanced pharmaceutical help.

"Mr Keller," she said, taking the chair opposite him. "Before we proceed, I need to confirm that you understand the preservation pathway and its limits."

"I've read the brochure."

"Then you understand that recovery cannot be guaranteed."

"You're all very keen on that point."

She inclined her head as if that counted as humour. "It matters."

"So does this. What happens if revival becomes possible but memory integrity doesn't?"

The technician stopped adjusting a lead near Adrian's collarbone. The physician answered without looking at him.

"There are no established frameworks for adjudicating that outcome."

"What about legal continuity if the world on the other side barely resembles the one I paid into?"

"There are estate-continuity provisions for preserved clients, but those are necessarily subject to—"

"That's not what I asked."

His mother had not moved from the wall. Adrian could feel her attention shift, which in this case meant she had gone from worried to watchful.

The physician paused. "The legal landscape around revival is still theoretical."

"Right."

"What we can say is that structural preservation success is possible under controlled conditions."

Adrian looked at her. "I'm not trying to preserve a ham sandwich."

That got a noise out of his mother, not quite a laugh but near enough. The technician looked down very quickly at the monitor unit in his hand.

To her credit, the physician did not get flustered for more than a second. "What I mean is that the probability space is not zero."

"Yes," Adrian said. "That's why I'm here. If it was zero, I'd have stayed home and died cheaper."

After that they dropped most of the brochure language and stuck to the procedure, which helped.

At the final checkpoint they let his mother come with him as far as the preparation room. It was smaller than he had expected, all stainless steel, frosted cabinet doors, and a reclining preservation cradle behind a clear partition, presented with enough empty space around it to make it look less like medical equipment and more like an expensive exhibit for people trying not to think too hard about death. His overnight bag sat on a chair. He had packed it three days earlier on principle, despite the fact that if this went well the contents would become archaeological trivia.

His mother picked the bag up, unzipped the outer pocket, and checked inside as if Helix might somehow have misplaced the future without her noticing.

"You're doing it again," he said.

"Yes."

"There's no version of this where I wake up and immediately need lip balm."

"You don't know that."

"I feel unusually confident."

She zipped the bag, set it down, and smoothed a crease that was not there on his sleeve. Then she checked the chair, the bag, and the small table with the dead-end paperwork on it as if there were still some practical mistake left to catch. Adrian waited for one of them to say something big. When neither of them did, he was glad in a way that felt smaller than the moment deserved. He just wanted something true.

"I was supposed to have longer," he said.

Her face tightened once, which was about as far as either of them was going to take it. "I know."

A knock came at the door, followed by an attendant with the careful posture of someone interrupting a funeral before it had officially started. "We're ready."

Adrian looked at the cradle, then back at his mother. She stepped in, touched the side of his face for less than a second, and let her hand fall again.

"If it works," she said, "be difficult there too."

He laughed. It came out wrong. "That was always the plan."

Then he went in.

The cradle was colder than it looked. Restraints settled across his arms and chest, light enough to feel polite, which somehow made them worse. A technician explained the first sedative pass while someone else adjusted the interface pad near his temple. Above him, the inner lid held a thin strip of blue light and a Helix logo so clean it made him want to complain on principle.

His hand moved before the thought was fully formed, fingers finding the edge of the cradle and holding there. The technician noticed and did not say anything. He could still get out — it was a stupid thought, but it was a completely serious one. He let his hand drop. He closed his eyes. Hand cream. Filtered air. The faint electrical hum beneath the room. No, that was the whole bloody point. He was done sitting politely inside a life that kept shrinking.

A voice asked him to count backwards from ten. He made it to seven before the room started going soft at the edges. At six he thought of rail lines vanishing into bad weather. At five he thought of wet concrete, broken fencing, and all the ordinary half-rotten places he had meant to revisit once he got better. At four he thought of his mother's hand on the car door, opening it before he had to fail at something simple in public. At three he was still thinking about the scarf. At two he tried to move his fingers and the thought went somewhere else first. At one the blue light narrowed to a white seam.

Then there was nothing.
