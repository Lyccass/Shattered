## Chapter 6 - A less meaningful conversation

"Fuck."

The curse tore out of Adrian before thought caught up. The knife had not snapped cleanly. The blade had broken halfway down and left him with a splintered handle and a jagged stump in his fist while the rest stayed buried somewhere under the beast's jaw. For one second his hand wouldn't move — still clenched around a useless stump, the message from his brain not arriving, because that was not how knives were supposed to behave and because the boar-thing was still very much alive on top of him.

Then it jerked.

Its head snapped sideways so hard it nearly tore his wrist with it. Hot breath burst across his face, wet and foul enough to make him gag, and the full weight of the thing slammed down again as it fought to get its front legs back under itself. Adrian jammed his forearm into the side of its neck and felt the muscle there bunch and writhe under the scales. One tusk punched into the ground beside his shoulder deep enough to throw dirt across his face. If the angle had been half an inch different, it would have gone into him instead.

He drove the broken stump down again and again. It bit, slipped, then tore free with almost no resistance. Useless. The boar-beast squealed and surged harder, dragging them both another foot downslope through grass and loose stone. Adrian's injured ribs lit up so sharply that his vision blurred for a second, and when he tried to get a knee under himself the animal's weight smashed it flat again before he could do anything useful with it.

He still had the broken handle clenched in one hand. His other was buried in the bristled fold beneath the beast's jaw, fingers slick with blood and pressed against the torn gap where the buried piece of blade had vanished into flesh. In blind desperation he stopped trying to stab and wrenched sideways instead, dragging hard on the wound and the trapped metal hidden inside it.

The beast let out a harsh, tearing scream and bucked so violently that some of its weight finally came off his chest. Adrian twisted with it, half rolling, half dragged, and suddenly they were no longer one tusk-length from his throat.

It still was not enough. The boar's forelegs scrabbled for purchase, tearing strips through the grass as it fought to turn back into him, and Adrian felt a fresh spike of panic when he realised that one good shift of weight would put those tusks back in line with his throat. He still had the broken handle in his fist, still had the beast twisted just badly enough to keep it from finishing the job, and if he lost that now the small advantage he had bought himself would vanish at once.

"For fuck's sake, stay down," he snarled, though the words came out more desperate than angry.

So he did the only thing left that even resembled a plan. Adrian planted his heel harder into the slope, dragged on the torn flesh with one hand, and drove his shoulder into the side of the beast's head as he wrenched sideways again, trying less to overpower it than to keep it twisted, off-balance, and unable to line those tusks up with anything vital.

The movement worked just enough. The boar thrashed, slipped, and came down badly on its front leg. Adrian felt the shift more than saw it. He brought the broken knife down into the same ruined gap under its jaw and stabbed again, then again, sawing and shoving with the jagged stump while the beast kicked and screamed and slowly lost what little control it had left. Blood ran hot over his fingers. He did not have the strength to overpower it, only enough to keep pressing into the damage already there and hope the body beneath him gave out first.

One last surge through the animal, hard enough to jolt his arms. A weaker one after that. Then nothing — the weight still there but changed, dead mass and failing muscle where there had been something trying to kill him.

For half a second Adrian just stayed there with the broken knife clenched in one hand and the other buried in blood-wet bristles, not trusting the stillness enough to believe it. Then a rush of heat hit beneath his sternum.

It came hard and deep, not surface warmth but something that hit the bone and spread outward, like the centre of him had been replaced with a burning coal and he was only now being told about it. Adrian sucked in a sharp breath and nearly choked on it. Every muscle in his body tightened at once. The rush was worse than adrenaline and stranger than pain, a violent pulse that flooded through his limbs and made his hands shake even harder than they already had.

The dead beast still crushed him into the hillside, its weight still across his chest and one trapped leg, like a collapsed wall of meat and scale, and for a second Adrian could do nothing except lie there under it and drag air into lungs that did not seem interested in cooperating. Then instinct took over again. He shoved at the carcass with both hands, planted one heel into the slope, and forced himself sideways with a low, furious groan that did not sound like human speech.

The body rolled just enough for him to wrench one leg free. Adrian twisted hard, nearly lost his grip on the broken knife, then rammed a shoulder into the beast's flank and kept pushing until the carcass finally tipped off him and thudded into the grass beside him. He ended up on one elbow and one knee, bent over, breathing through clenched teeth while his whole body trembled with effort, pain, and whatever the hell that rush inside him was doing. He was still shaking. He was also, somehow, grinning. He stopped when he noticed.

That was when the sound reached him.

Voices from downslope cut across the wind, sharp and sudden and far too close now. More than one. Human. Moving fast. Adrian lifted his head, blood still running over his fingers, and felt the whole situation turn on him before he had even managed to get properly upright.

*Oh, for fuck's sake. Of course they heard that.*

He made the decision in about a second, which was all the time available.

Running was not viable. His knee had opinions about running. His ribs had louder ones. The slope would turn a serious sprint into a controlled tumble before he covered fifty yards, and even if he managed to stay upright, six people who knew this land would close the distance before he found better ground. He was also coated in blood from wrist to chin and standing directly over the carcass of an armoured pig the size of a small car. Running from that did not look innocent. It looked like something a guilty man did. Which was arguably accurate, but not the point.

*Stand still. Look harmless. You have seen worse mornings. You have not, actually, but the thought is useful.*

He wiped his hands on his coat — already ruined, which was at least one problem solved — and straightened as far as his ribs would let him before tucking the broken handle into his pocket where it wouldn't be the first thing they saw.

The voices came over the slope thirty seconds later.

The first figure appeared over the rise at about thirty yards, and the rest followed in that same loose practical spacing he had watched from above. Six of them, armed and not casual about it. One had an arrow already nocked. Another had a hand resting on a sword hilt without drawing it, which he decided to read as moderately encouraging. The big man with the mismatched shoulder guard stopped on the rise and looked at Adrian, then at the boar, then back at Adrian with an expression that made no promises.

Adrian did not move. He was not sure he trusted his legs to move.

He raised both hands slowly to shoulder height. Empty palms. Universal gesture, he hoped, because if it meant something aggressive in whatever language these people spoke he was going to have a very short remaining afternoon. Nobody drew on him. Progress.

The woman in the mended coat spoke first. Her words meant nothing. He watched her face instead and got suspicion and the particular attention of someone who needed information more than they needed a fight.

"I can't understand you," Adrian said, keeping his voice level. "I'm sorry. I genuinely cannot understand a single word."

The phrasing changed. Still nothing he could use.

He gestured at himself, then at the boar, then spread both hands — it probably looked like he was miming a theatrical retelling of the fight, and there was no version of correcting that in real time.

"I killed it," he said, because there was a stupid persistent part of the brain that kept trying. "The pig. I killed the pig. I was not doing anything else out here. I am not a threat. I am having a significantly worse day than you."

The arrow had not moved.

The big man said something short to the others. Two of them adjusted their positions in response, spreading wider along the slope in a way that was not aggressive exactly, but was not encouraging either. *Flanking. They are actually flanking me over a dead pig.*

After a long moment, the woman in the mended coat stepped forward from the group and crouched beside the boar. She examined it with the brisk, assessing look of someone checking damage rather than displaying concern. She turned the broken jaw with two fingers, noted the angle of entry, looked at the blood pooled in the grass. Then she stood and said something to the others with a changed inflection. Still cautious, but the alarm had gone out of it.

Adrian let himself breathe properly for the first time in several minutes.

*Progress. Someone has decided I am probably not the monster here. Work with that.*

He crouched beside the carcass himself, slowly, keeping both hands in plain sight. He took the broken knife handle from his coat and set it on the grass in front of him. Then he pointed at the wound. Then at himself. Then at the knife. He mimed, as clearly as he could manage, the knife breaking in his hand.

This produced some kind of exchange among the group. The words were useless, so he read faces — the young one near the back asking the same question twice, the woman answering with the finality of someone closing a door, the big man's hand finally coming off the sword hilt.

The standoff settled into something more like an impasse.

*We have established that I am probably not hostile and that I did in fact kill the armoured pig with a knife that did not survive the experience. What we have not established is what happens now, because nobody here speaks anything I can use and I am running out of mime options.*

He was trying to decide whether pointing upslope and walking would convey *I will simply leave* or *I am retreating suspiciously* when footsteps came from above.

Not a charge. A walk. Unhurried in a way that felt almost pointed, as if urgency were a thing beneath the walker's notice.

The group's attention shifted upslope before Adrian had fully processed the sound. The archer lowered his aim slightly — not all the way, but enough to be deliberate. The woman in the mended coat said something in a tone that contained a name in it. The big man relaxed his shoulders by maybe a fraction.

Adrian turned.

The man coming down from the higher ground moved like the land owed him nothing — lean and at a pace that had nothing to hurry in it, wearing gear that struck Adrian as slightly too clean for frontier work, though he couldn't immediately say why. His hands were loose at his sides, and nothing in his face or posture was doing the work of looking relaxed — he simply looked like a man to whom this particular situation did not register as a problem.

He spoke to the group as he descended, a few easy sentences, and whatever he said produced visible results. The arrow came down. The big man moved back. Whatever he had said, the slope stopped feeling like the few seconds before something went wrong.

Then the man looked at Adrian.

It was not an unfriendly look. It was careful and thorough and it lasted about three seconds, which was a second and a half longer than most looks felt comfortable staying. The kind of look that took inventory rather than formed an impression.

"You are from very far away," he said.

English. Accented in a way Adrian could not place against anything he had ever heard, but English, delivered with the ease of someone reaching for something they had not recently used rather than something they had needed to learn.

"You speak English," he said.

"I collect things," the man said, which was not an explanation.

"That is—" Adrian stopped. He had several questions. He chose the most immediately useful one. "How?"

"There are more useful questions," the man said, and looked at the dead boar. One eyebrow moved. "You killed that with a broken knife."

"I also used a spear."

"You lost it halfway."

"That — how do you know that?"

"I watched."

He held that for a second. Keiran had been up there the whole time. "You watched."

"Yes."

"I see."

"Good, eyes are working also."

Adrian took a breath. "You could have helped, you know."

"I know."

"Why didn't you?"

"You looked like you had everything under control."

"I did?"

"No." The word was flat, but there was something underneath it. "But I suspected it would work out for you." He looked back at Adrian. "You are injured."

"I had to fight a scaled boar. Of course I am injured."

"Mm." He crouched beside the boar in the same efficient way the woman had used, turned the broken jaw, looked at the wound. Then he stood. "Old growth scales. This one had been getting away from things long enough to build real mass." He glanced at Adrian, then gestured over his shoulder toward the six people still standing awkwardly upslope. "You are upright. That is already better than several results I have seen from this particular stretch."

"I almost died. What do you want to say with that?"

"It is an observation."

The group had stayed where they were, watching the exchange with the particular attention of people who could not follow the words but could follow the tone. The woman in the mended coat was looking at the man with an expression that suggested she was used to this and had feelings about it.

The man turned and spoke to the group again. More back and forth followed. Adrian caught the shape of authority in it — not the kind that issued orders and expected silence in return, but the kind that had enough weight to make arguments short. The woman asked something pointed. He answered. The big man with the shoulder guard looked at Adrian once more, then apparently decided his reservations could be filed without resolution.

After a moment the man turned back.

"They're not sure what to do with you. The blood doesn't help, but that's the smaller problem." He didn't elaborate on what the larger one was.

"I can explain the blood."

"You already did." He gestured at the boar. "They are not unreasonable. They simply have no useful frame for what you are."

"Neither do I, frankly." He glanced at the group — still watching, still keeping their distance — and then back at Keiran. "What did you tell them?"

"That you came through the old route in the hill and that the confusion is understandable. That the boar is three seasons worth of problem off their patrol stretch. That you are not hostile." He paused. "That the language gap is mine to manage and they should treat you as a traveller until further evidence suggests otherwise."

"And they accepted that."

"They accepted it provisionally." Something in his expression was careful and a little dry. "I have a certain amount of goodwill in this area."

Adrian looked at him for a moment. "Why?"

The man considered this the way someone might consider an unusual coin — briefly, with genuine interest, and without urgency.

"The route you came through has been dead for longer than this country has had a name," he said. "And you arrived on foot, carrying salvage and a spear, with no escort, no local knowledge, and enough practical sense to assess a group of armed strangers before walking into range." He glanced once, briefly, at the boar. "And then you killed something that should, by any fair measure, have killed you first."

"That part was mostly desperation."

"You had your fingers in the wound when I came over the rise. That was not desperation. That was a decision." He looked back at Adrian. "That is why."

Adrian filed it. He'd know which one it was when it mattered, or he wouldn't.

"You have a name," Adrian said.

"Keiran," the man said. "Route work. Among other things."

*Among other things* was doing considerable work in that sentence. Adrian filed it alongside *I collect things* as things that wanted examining when he had more ground under his feet.

"Adrian," he said. "Surveyor. A long time ago."

Something shifted in Keiran's expression. Too brief and too controlled to be called a reaction. He turned the word over the same way he had examined the boar's jaw — thoroughly, and with the air of someone reaching a conclusion they were not going to share immediately.

"A surveyor," he said.

"Infrastructure. Transit. Remote systems." Adrian paused. "It doesn't translate well."

"More than you might think," Keiran said, which also wanted examining. He did not expand on it.

The group was beginning to shift. Nothing dramatic — a gradual gathering of weight and attention that suggested they had been stopped long enough and the light was still sinking. The woman in the mended coat said something in Keiran's direction. He answered without looking away from Adrian.

"They are continuing toward the waystation below the ridge," he said. "Approximately two hours. Defensible. Supplied." He looked at Adrian's coat, his hands, the broken knife in his pocket. "You need water, time for—" He glanced at Adrian's ribs. "And a night that's not out here."

"And in exchange?"

Keiran's expression remained exactly the same. "The boar is a resource, with the right tools. They do not have everything needed to process it here. But the location is noted." He glanced at the carcass. "And you have demonstrated you are not entirely incompetent, which is the minimum threshold for company in this part of the world."

He didn't have enough ground under him yet to know which it was. He picked up the broken knife handle, turned it once — the broken end was useless and the weight wrong — and put it back in his pocket. Leaving it would have felt like giving up on the question of whether it had been a good knife.

"All right," he said.

They started downslope.

Keiran walked beside him without the deliberateness of someone managing a situation. He simply walked. The group kept their spacing ahead and the same instinctive attention Adrian had noticed before remained, but with less weight in it now. The archer had his bow across his back.

After a few minutes of descent in which nothing was said and the wind came low and cold across the grass, Adrian spoke.

"How long have you been watching?"

"Since the arch," Keiran said, without any notable change in tone.

"The arch."

"Yes."

Adrian thought about that for a moment. He had come through the arch, climbed a rise, found a path marker, followed the group, got charged by an armoured pig, and fought it for what felt like a very long time before eventually killing it. The man walking beside him had apparently observed the entirety of this.

"You watched for that long," Adrian said. "Before coming down."

"You did not need assistance."

"The boar was on top of me."

"You had the situation under control."

"I had a broken knife jammed in the situation's jaw."

"Yes," Keiran said. "That was the part I was particularly enjoying."

There was no useful response to that. Adrian let it go and watched the country below, where the waystation would be, wherever it was. The light had gone golden and flat across the hillside and the mountains were darker in the distance than they had been an hour ago.

He had no read on this man, no read on this land, one night's water left, ribs that were making their case loudly, and the only leverage he had was a dead pig.

He adjusted his grip on the spear and kept walking.
