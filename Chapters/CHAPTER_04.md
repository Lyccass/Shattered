# Chapter 4 - Aftermath

Then the level settled in properly.

Heat pushed out from the Runeheart beneath Adrian's sternum, not with the crushing violence of the class selection but with a slow, insistent force that spread through shoulders, forearms, spine, hips, and thighs and made every muscle lock for half a heartbeat before releasing. He sucked in a breath. The maintenance pipe in his hands felt different at once. Still bent. Still badly balanced. Still a desperate bit of salvage rather than a real weapon. But no longer like something he was barely forcing into obedience through panic and luck. His grip bit deeper. His footing felt fractionally clearer. Even with blood running warm under his coat and his knee threatening complaints, there was simply more of him there than there had been a second ago.

*The numbers had done that.*

Then the last one twitched.

Adrian flinched and drove the end of the maintenance pipe back into its side hard enough to shove the body a few inches across the floor. The thing made a wet scraping noise against the metal plates and then, finally, stayed still. He kept the pipe there for another second anyway, leaning on it harder than he needed to, because he did not yet trust stillness in this place and had even less reason to trust anything with that many teeth.

Only when nothing else moved did the rest of the room come back into focus. The bay had gone quiet so abruptly that the silence felt wrong, as though something important had been cut out of the air and the room had not caught up yet. He could hear his own breathing, too loud and too fast, and the steady hum from the arch chamber beyond the wall. Over that sat the smell: hot blood on metal, old dust, the sour edge of his own fear. His gaze kept catching on ugly little details he had not asked for, on blood drying tacky between his fingers, on the red smear down the pipe, on the bench leg spattered with something that had not been there a minute ago.

"That," he said hoarsely into the empty bay, "was deeply unpleasant."

He tried to lower the pipe and discovered his hands were shaking hard enough to rattle it against the floor.

That got through to him more than the bodies had. Not the killing, exactly, or even the part where one of the things had nearly gotten into his ribs. It was the delayed, stupidly human aftermath, the point where his body apparently decided the emergency notice could finally go out because the immediate danger had stopped trying to eat him. He looked down at his hands, then at his left leg, then back at his hands because one check clearly was not going to satisfy whatever frantic little inspector had taken over his nervous system. It was all still working. His knee hurt, his side burned, and his pulse felt as though it had moved into his throat, but when he had needed to move, he had moved. There had been no lag, no hateful dead patch between intention and response, no familiar moment where his nerves seemed to pause and consider whether they could be bothered. He had been slow because he was scared, clumsy because he was untrained, and nearly dead because he had gone monster-hunting with a length of pipe in a cupboard-sized room. He had not been slow because his own body had betrayed him.

The relief came off that realisation so hard it nearly folded him.

He sat on the bench before the floor could make the decision for him. The metal groaned under his weight, and the maintenance pipe slipped from his fingers and clanged against the leg with a noise far too loud for the room. Adrian bent forward at once and tried to drag a cleaner breath in. That should not have been difficult. Breathe in, breathe out, repeat until adulthood resumed. Instead his lungs kept yanking in air too fast, as though they had formed their own opinion on how this ought to go, and every deeper breath tugged at the claw marks along his ribs hard enough to make him wince.

"Brilliant," he heard himself say. "Perfect. Love this."

That did not help. Neither did shutting his eyes. The darkness behind them only gave his head more room to throw things at him: the cradle, the arch, the floating text, the corpse in the locker, the things in the wall, the hot inward pull each kill had fed beneath his sternum. *What the fuck was happening?* *How long had he been here?* *Why were there monsters in the walls of a dead cryonics facility?* *Why was any of this real?*

His chest tightened harder. For one ugly second he thought he might actually be sick. For another he thought he might laugh, and that was worse somehow. Laughing on a maintenance bench beside three dead not-geckos while a giant black ring woke up in the next room would have been a bad look even in private. *Nope.* Absolutely not. If he let himself tip over into hysterics now, whether that came out as laughter or something else, he was going to end up curled over on a bench in a dead facility while the rest of the ruin carried on being a nightmare around him. That did not solve anything. It did not get him water. It did not get him out. It did not stop whatever lived in the walls from coming back.

He opened his eyes, stared at the floor, and pressed his palms harder into the edge of the bench until the sting gave the rest of his body something smaller to complain about. Count it. In for four, hold it, out for four. His lungs ignored the first attempt. The second got to three before his breath hitched. On the third try he managed the full cycle, though only by resenting it with the kind of focus physiotherapy had once demanded on a weekly basis.

That brought the spiral down from catastrophic to merely vicious. Enough to think, not enough to feel normal. Normal had left hours ago. Maybe centuries ago. No useful version of the problem involved him suddenly understanding all of this in one elegant insight. Helix had been real. The facility was real. The arch was real. The system text was either real or the most aggressively committed hallucination in human history, and three cooling bodies on the floor strongly suggested the distinction did not matter. Fine. He could hate that later.

What mattered right now was that he was alive, thirsty, bleeding, under-armed, and still inside a ruin that had just demonstrated a strong interest in producing teeth. Getting out did not start with sprinting blindly into the corridor while shaking hard enough to miss the door. It started with becoming slightly less easy to kill. Water. Food. Something better than the pipe. Something for the wounds. Then information. Then a way out.

"Get a grip," he muttered, hearing how thin his own voice sounded and disliking it. "Any grip. Borrow one if you have to."

That might have been funny under better circumstances. Under current circumstances it was simply practical advice, and it helped that thirst arrived immediately afterwards and reduced the world to a set of problems he could actually do something about. His mouth had gone dry enough to feel scraped out, hunger followed a heartbeat later with vindictive efficiency, and his right knee had started lodging formal complaints every time he shifted. Fine. Those were useful problems: water, food, a better weapon, something for the wounds. If he kept going one thing at a time, he might stop his brain from trying to sprint in six directions at once.

Once his breathing had come down to something vaguely compatible with function again, he dragged the status back up because numbers, for all their faults, were at least something he could look at without spiralling.

*[Status]*

*Name: Adrian Keller*
*Stage: Iron*
*Level: 2*
*Class: Fighter*
*Trait: Empty*

*Strength: 6*
*Agility: 4*
*Endurance: 6*
*Sense: 3*
*Will: 6*
*Pulse: 4*

*Unassigned Points: 1*
*Technique: None*

He blinked until the text stopped swimming. It matched what he had seen a minute ago. Strength had gone from five to six. Agility had climbed from three to four. Endurance had done the same. Pulse had followed them. Sense and Will had not moved at all, and the system had apparently decided the whole ugly business was also worth one free point on top.

He looked from the numbers to his own hand around the pipe and squeezed experimentally. The proof was still there. The ache in his forearm was real, but the grip underneath it felt denser somehow, less near its limit than it should have after a fight, a panic spiral, and blood loss. He planted his boots, stood, then crouched again beside the bench and came back up. His ribs protested. His knee lodged a complaint. The movement still went through cleanly. Not painless. Not graceful. Just stronger. More there.

For a second he simply stared at himself.

The system had not updated a form. It had put more actual body under his skin, and it had left him one spare choice on top of that.

So there was a pattern, or at least the outline of one. Fighter was not some vague promise of general usefulness; it looked more like an aggressively opinionated investment strategy that wanted him to hit harder, stay upright longer, move a little better, and get more of whatever Pulse was supposed to be, then spend the extra point himself and decide which flaw was currently trying hardest to kill him. Technique still sat there as *None*, which irritated him, but at the moment he had no evidence that a Technique would do him more good than water, bandages, and something longer than a pipe.

Which meant one point was not theoretical. It was not some background modifier quietly making hidden calculations on his behalf. Strength meant his grip had actual bite behind it now. Endurance meant he was still upright after the sort of minute that should have left him flattened and making poor life choices. Agility, limited though it was, meant his body was at least meeting him halfway. The trouble was that he still had no real sense of scale. One point was clearly noticeable. But was it small by this world's standards? Normal? Huge? He had exactly one fight's worth of data, and that fight had mostly established that surprise, cramped spaces, and inadequate equipment made for poor testing conditions.

Pulse remained the awkward line. The more he stared at that number, the less he could pretend the heat under his sternum during the fight had been simple adrenaline. Every kill had fed it. He had felt that happen far too clearly to explain it away. That meant Pulse was some kind of energy, or fuel, or resource, or whatever term this world preferred once ordinary life stopped applying and nonsense gained administrative backing. He did not love the name. Pulse sounded less like a fundamental force and more like something dreamt up in a branding meeting.

Even that irritated him less than the Trait line did. The word *Empty* sat there like a bad joke, and he focused on it again, partly because he was stubborn and partly because annoyance was doing useful work keeping worse emotions out.

*[Trait: Empty]*
*[No seeded Trait present.]*

The answer remained exactly as useless as before. If everyone else started with some kind of seeded Trait and he did not, then he was already behind before he had even seen another living person in this place. Assuming there were other people. Assuming the next thing he met qualified as people. Either way, being the exception in the bad direction was not the sort of luck he had been hoping the new universe might bring with it.

He kept staring at the word for another moment, as if sheer irritation might force more information out of it, but the system had shown no particular interest in being helpful so far and was not about to start now.

"Right," he said softly, because the silence in the bay had become too close and he needed to hear an actual voice, even if it was only his own. "Empty. Brilliant. Not worrying at all."

He dismissed the screen before he could keep picking at it like a loose tooth. If he sat there much longer, he was going to start replaying the fight, then the waking, then the cradle, then the timeline, and somewhere in the middle of that his brain would decide this was the ideal moment for a complete collapse. He could do that later if it still sounded productive. Right now he needed something in the room he could actually change, so he made himself look around properly.

The dead worker by the lockers had not moved, which Adrian appreciated. The emergency lamp still threw its thin, jaundiced light across the bay, catching on tool marks and old scuffs. Near the floor, beside the panel he had noticed before everything with claws interrupted him, the seam looked wrong in the deliberate way wear sometimes did. It had been opened more than once, used and maintained by somebody who had expected to come back to it. If somebody had lived out of this bay for even a short stretch, they had not done it with one coat, a lamp, and optimism. People made caches when they had time, fear, access to tools, and very little interest in dying empty-handed. That was not comforting exactly, but it was at least legible. If there had ever been sensible people here, there was a decent chance one of them had hidden something useful.

He pushed himself up, hissed when the claw marks along his ribs pulled, and dug the token out of his pocket. It sat solid in his palm, dark metal worn smooth at the edges, the same unfamiliar script cut across one face, the three-point mark in one corner as if it ought to mean something obvious. He focused on it.

*[Service Token - Proven]*
*[Access token keyed to maintenance caches and utility locks within a degraded transit cluster. Authority partial. Function unstable.]*

Somewhere under the fear and fatigue, a small clean thread of satisfaction broke through.

"Now we're talking."

He tried the same trick with the rusted belt knife. Nothing. The emergency lamp gave him nothing as well, and neither did a dirty ration wrapper on the floor.

Useful, then, but selective. The system would label things that mattered and leave the rest to the older, less glamorous method of looking at an object and making a guess. Fair enough.

He crouched beside the floor panel and found that the notch by the seam matched the token's shape suspiciously well. The world could manage monsters, system prompts, and impossible arches, but subtlety was apparently a bridge too far. He slid the token in and pressed. Something clicked under the plate, and the panel rose just enough for him to get his fingers beneath it.

Adrian let out a breath he had not realised he was holding and hauled the thing open. The compartment beneath had been built properly. Shallow sections sat fitted between structural ribs, each lined with old cloth so nothing could rattle or shift. Even after however much time had passed, the cache still looked organised, and that did more for him than it probably should have. Somebody here had panicked intelligently, which was oddly comforting.

Water sat right at the front, and the sight of it hit him hard enough that his hands nearly fumbled the bladder on the way up. He twisted the cap loose and drank before caution could object. The first swallow turned into three in quick succession, and he had to wrench the thing away from his own mouth and stand there with it in both hands while his stomach argued for more. Too fast and he was going to bring it straight back up, which would have been a spectacular waste.

He forced himself to go slower after that, taking smaller mouthfuls and waiting between them. The water tasted cold, metallic, and old enough that a more sensible version of him might have worried. Adrian settled for grateful. The headache behind his eyes eased first, and after another minute the room stopped swaying in that unpleasant half-step way it had been doing.

"Thank fuck," he whispered.

The ration bars came next, four of them in dull wrappers that looked as though they had been designed to survive the end of civilisation out of spite. Adrian focused on one out of habit.

Nothing.

Apparently even the future had limits to how much help it was willing to provide.

He opened a bar and bit in anyway. Salt, grain, and compressed disappointment. Awful. More importantly, edible. He chewed while staring down into the cache, waiting for his stomach to decide whether it was willing to cooperate. Half a bar later the answer became yes, reluctantly. The hard edge had gone out of the hunger, and his thoughts stopped arriving in one tangled rush. Better. Still absurd, still awful, still not even slightly normal, but better was enough to work with.

He went through the rest more carefully after that.

There was a sword in a plain scabbard, a hatchet, a hammer, a compact shield, bandages, a flat tin of sharply herbal salve, a second water bladder, and two wrapped long shafts tucked underneath the lot. Adrian reached for the sword first because some stubborn, optimistic part of his brain still wanted the answer to be obvious. He drew it, tested the balance, shifted his grip, and immediately pictured trying to use that much blade in this bay with his ribs already complaining and his knee one bad twist away from betrayal.

No. Not because the sword was bad. Because it assumed a man who knew what he was doing.

He gave it another second anyway, point low, wrist turning minutely as he tried to imagine the first cut, the recovery, the second cut if the first did not solve everything neatly like fiction insisted it should. Edge alignment. Distance. Timing. What happened when the creature got past the blade and into him. Swords wanted a relationship. Technique. Familiarity. Actual opinions. Adrian slid it back into the scabbard before he could insult them both.

The hatchet felt promising for exactly as long as it took him to picture the range. He hefted it once, liking the straightforward weight of it, then imagined one of the wall-things slipping past the first swing and arriving inside that short arc with teeth and claws and momentum. He put it back down at once. After the last few minutes, anything that required the enemy to stand politely within arm's reach felt less like courage and more like administrative error.

The hammer stayed in his hand a little longer. It had an honesty the others lacked. You hit something with it until the disagreement ended. Adrian respected that. Unfortunately it was top-heavy, short, and built for someone who trusted his footing, their recovery, and the general state of their skeleton considerably more than he currently trusted any of his.

The shield annoyed him most because he wanted it to work. More metal between himself and claws sounded like civilisation. He took it up, shifted his arm through the straps, and immediately felt how much attention it demanded. Positioning. Angles. What to cover and what to leave open. How to recover after a bad block. How to use it without losing track of the weapon in his other hand. It was not just a defence. It was another full set of decisions, and he was already spending too much effort managing the first set.

His hand touched wrapped wood at the bottom of the compartment, and some knot in the back of his shoulders eased before he had even fully pulled it free. Of course it was a spear. He unwrapped the head far enough to focus on it.

*[March Spear - Proven]*
*[Field combat spear used along unstable routes and salvage corridors. Built for reach, repeat use, and ugly work.]*

Adrian laughed under his breath, tired enough that the sound came out more like an exhale. "Ugly work," he murmured. "That I can do."

The thing made sense in his hands immediately. The shaft was dark wood worn smooth where somebody competent had used it before him, and the head was plain, leaf-shaped, and refreshingly free of pretence. It did not ask for finesse. It did not imply elegance. It asked him to keep the sharp end between himself and whatever wanted him dead, and at the moment that counted as admirable clarity.

He stood carefully and tried a slow thrust in the narrow bay. Then he tried another, paying attention to how the point went out, how it came back, and where his feet tried to betray him in between. He was still clumsy, still late on the recovery, still one poor step away from doing something terminally stupid, but the movement at least felt understandable. The pipe had already taught him the underlying lesson. Reach bought time. The spear was that lesson refined by someone who had known what they were doing.

And the spear was not light. That part mattered. An hour ago, even before the cryo coffin and the monsters and the architectural insult in the next room, his arms would have started their usual quiet negotiations much sooner. Now the shaft sat in his grip with solid, manageable weight rather than immediate threat. His shoulders still burned. His hands still shook a little. But the effort had shifted from impossible to merely difficult, and that was so obviously the stats that he almost resented needing the proof.

"Right," he said, adjusting his grip and squaring himself a little. "You and I have a chance."

He kept the knife as well, because there were going to be spaces too tight for the spear and dying for lack of a backup option would have been insulting. The sword, hatchet, hammer, and shield went back into the compartment, not because they were bad but because he was not the man to make good use of them yet.

The bandages and salve came out next. Once he sat back on the bench and peeled coat and preservation suit aside, the wounds looked uglier than they had in the middle of the fight. Three claw lines crossed his left ribs, shallow but open. Scratches along the collarbone had bled theatrically, and his right knee was already shading into a bruise that promised tomorrow would be educational in all the wrong ways.

Seeing the damage up close brought too much of the fight back with it. He saw again the first movement from the wall, heard the crack of pipe against bone or cartilage or whatever those things had possessed, and remembered with unpleasant clarity the moment the second creature got too close and he knew, with bright stupid certainty, that he was about to die in a storage bay because he had gone to inspect a floor panel. His stomach tightened. For half a second the bay seemed to shrink around him again, bench too close on one side, lockers on the other, nowhere clean to step if something else came boiling out of the wall.

*Too close.*

Adrian set the water down, took the spear into one hand on reflex, and waited. Nothing moved except the emergency lamp's weak shimmer across the floor and his own breathing. He made himself put the spear back across his knees.

"Still dead," he muttered towards the bodies. "Stay that way."

Then he looked very deliberately at the roll of bandage rather than the floor seam and kept his hands moving. Clean it, salve it, wrap it. He had spent too much of his old life around hospitals, home kits, improvised field fixes, and very calm professional instructions not to know the basic rule. If something important had opened up, the first job was making it less open. The first job after that was not thinking too hard about how close the important bits had come to being opened more.

The salve stung hard enough to drag a hiss out of him, which he accepted almost gratefully. Pain was simple. Pain was local. Pain was infinitely preferable to letting his thoughts start running loose again. He could panic after he was bandaged. Preferably somewhere with a door he understood.

By the time he finished, the wounds still burned, still tugged when he breathed too deeply, and still felt like a warning written directly onto his skin. They had moved from immediate problem to managed problem, though, and that would have to do.

The spear lay across his knees while he sat there breathing through the last of the aftershocks. His hands had mostly stopped shaking, though one still twitched now and then if his gaze strayed to the bodies or if the bay got too quiet for comfort. He opened the status again before his mind found something less useful to obsess over, and the free point still waited there with calm administrative patience.

He had more information now than he had ten minutes earlier, which was not the same thing as understanding but at least pointed in the same direction. Strength had done its part. He could already hit hard enough to kill if he found the opening. Endurance had kept him moving after being cut. Will remained oddly high and completely uninterested in explaining itself. Sense had a respectable case after three wall-things had nearly gutted him.

And yet every time he replayed the fight in his head, what came back was not the lack of force. The force had been real. So had the extra engine under his skin now. It was the mess in his movement that kept trying to get him killed. He had lost ground whenever the range collapsed, whenever his footing lied to him, whenever panic picked his next step before he did. The spear only made that clearer. If he wanted to survive another fight, he needed to be able to get the point out, get it back, and move without his own feet turning traitor in the middle of the exchange. Another point of Strength would help when he was already doing something right. Another point of Endurance would let him survive being wrong. What he needed more urgently was to be wrong less often.

He scratched at his jaw with the heel of one hand and looked down at his boots, at the angle of his legs, at the way his weight still wanted to sit badly when he stopped paying attention. The answer felt less like insight than admission. Agility, then.

"If this makes me even slightly less embarrassing," he muttered, "I'll count it as a win," and selected the point.

The change came nothing like the class selection had. There was no crushing heat this time, no violent pressure through his chest, only a small strange tightening through hips, shoulders, ankles, and wrists, as if some leftover slack he had not known was there had been quietly drawn in and tied off. He sucked in a breath when his balance shifted under him, the sensation brief and deeply odd, like someone had reached inside his frame and straightened a set of parts that had been sitting half a degree out of true.

Adrian stood, shifted his grip on the spear, and walked a few careful steps across the bay. The first step landed where he meant it. So did the second. When he turned, there was less of that old internal negotiation, less of the tiny argument between intention and body. He came back, planted, and thrust once at the far wall. The point went out straighter. It came back cleaner. He did it again, this time stepping in a little harder, and his front foot actually arrived under him rather than somewhere adjacent to the concept.

He stopped and blinked.

That was not training. He had not secretly become competent in the last thirty seconds. The system had just reached in and made movement easier.

He tried one more time with a little more commitment in the front step, and that, at least, felt like progress rather than wishful thinking. He still had no idea what he was doing in any formal sense, but his body seemed slightly less inclined to object while he learned.

The ruin chose that moment to remind him he was not alone in it. A heavier vibration rolled through the bay and made the lamp flicker. The hum from the arch deepened until he could feel it through the spear shaft, and Adrian was on his feet and at the viewing slit before he had consciously decided to move.

The chamber beyond had changed while he was busy bleeding, drinking, and trying to become marginally less useless. Blue-white light now ran through most of the grooves around the ring. The membrane in its centre had thickened from suggestion into something almost substantial, like frozen water deciding it wanted to become glass, and a narrow seam of light had opened along the far wall where there had definitely not been one earlier.

The token in his pocket had gone faintly warm. He took it out, frowned at it, and was still looking between the metal disc and the brightening arch when a sharp ring of metal sounded somewhere deeper in the ruin. It was not close, and it did not sound random either. A second noise followed a moment later, blurred by distance into something that might have been a voice or might have been machinery doing a poor imitation of one.

Adrian held still and listened with the spear half raised.

Nothing came after that except the hum of waking systems, the occasional tick of old metal taking strain, and the low uncomfortable sense that the place around him was remembering how to function. It could have been survivors, or something with hands, or a door somewhere finally deciding to act like a door again, and none of those possibilities made the room around him feel any safer.

The bay felt smaller now, and not just because he knew what had come out of the walls. It had four walls, one real exit, three dead things on the floor, and exactly no room to manoeuvre if whatever was waking up outside decided to come in. Staying because it was familiar would have been the same mistake as freezing in the middle of a fight simply because moving hurt.

He capped the water bladder, adjusted his grip on the spear, and gave the room one last look: bench, lockers, hatch, bodies, cache. The place had done what it could for him, and he was leaving it alive, armed, and only moderately bleeding, which by current standards probably counted as success.

"Brilliant," he said quietly. "Glowing arch. Opening wall. Maybe voices. Exactly what I wanted."

Then he stepped out to find which problem had opened first.
