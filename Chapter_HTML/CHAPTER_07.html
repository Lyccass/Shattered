The waystation sat low against the hillside, squat dark stone half-hidden by the slope — built to hold heat and deny sight-lines, not to welcome anyone.

He went through the door last, waited until the heavy slab of it had thudded shut behind him, and put his back to the wall where he could see the hearth, the entrance, and most of the room without needing to move much. The choice had as much to do with caution as exhaustion. His body had been asking him to stop for the better part of the past hour. The pull in his ribs had settled into a steady warning that sharpened every time he forgot and breathed too deeply. His knee felt hot and uncertain under his weight, as if one bad twist might make an argument of it. If someone had pointed at the floor and told him to sit, he might have done it on gratitude alone.

The room carried the signs of recent use. A banked fire waited in the hearth with enough life left in it to wake quickly. Split wood sat stacked beside it under waxed cloth. Supplies lined one wall in neat practical bundles, the kind of arrangement made by people who expected to need them in bad light and with cold hands. The floor had been swept within the last few days, which meant either discipline or enough foot traffic to make filth inconvenient — either way this was no abandoned ruin people occasionally borrowed, but a working stop on a route that had enough traffic to matter. If this was a hallucination, it had an exhausting commitment to texture, and at some point the distinction stopped mattering.

The others settled into it with the kind of practised caution that still showed its seams. One boy took the door and checked the latch before setting himself beside it. Two more knelt by the hearth — one glanced at the other before touching flame to kindling, a small check, the kind people made when the order of things hadn't been spoken aloud yet but everyone expected it to exist. The woman in the mended coat worked through the stores by touch as much as sight, counting them without looking up, as though the number was the only thing that needed verifying. The big man disappeared through a low back door without asking anyone whether he should. Nobody ignored Adrian by accident. Every time somebody crossed the room, their eyes clipped over him for a beat. The group had decided, with the studied quiet of people on unfamiliar ground, that the stranger could remain so long as he remained legible.

He stayed where he was and let his breathing settle, counting faces while it did. On the slope they had looked like a competent armed group and not much more. In here, with firelight catching faces and the edge worn out of distance, the impression sharpened into something more specific.

They were young. Not in the vague sense where anyone under forty looked young when your day had involved cryostasis, teleportation, and a mutant boar. Actually young. Firelight did them no favours there. It flattened the harder expressions and brought out the unfinished parts. The archer could not have been much past seventeen. The boy who had tried to explain things earlier looked younger than that whenever he forgot to keep his face arranged. Even the big man, broad as he was, had the half-settled solidity of someone who had grown fast recently and hadn't quite worked out what to do with the size.

What they lacked was not competence so much as settled experience. They moved carefully. They knew where hands, weapons, and eyes were supposed to go. But Adrian could still see the corrections: a hand that checked a strap twice, a look to the side before someone committed to the next small task. Nothing had sunk deep enough to become invisible yet. This had to be one of their first real runs. They were not children playing at being dangerous. They were young people doing serious work for probably the first time, taking pains to look as though it was already normal.

The big man returned, set a cloth-wrapped bundle on the stone by the hearth, and unrolled it. Dark meat, leaner than pork, faint bluish tinge at one cut edge. Not the boar. Something else from a food chain Adrian was in no position to map.

The big man straightened, rolled one shoulder as if easing old strain out of it, and looked round the room before his eyes landed on Adrian. He did not look at him like a host offering comfort or even like a leader making a point. It was closer to the look someone gave a tool they had not used before but were willing to try once. Then he nudged the bundle across the stone with two fingers — not offering it, exactly. More like placing it in front of someone and waiting to see what they'd do with it.

It could have been hospitality, a test, or the plain logic that spare hands ought to earn their heat. Adrian had no way to choose between them, which meant it made sense to behave as if all three were true. He took the meat, nodded once so the movement could stand in for thanks, agreement, and willingness all at once, and crouched by the fire with a borrowed knife while six sets of attention hovered around the edge of his vision. The whole arrangement was slightly absurd, which did nothing to make it less real.

He had cooked for himself for years before that stopped being worth the effort, which meant he knew at least how not to starve — though none of that had included a heated stone and whatever the March counted as game.

The stone by the hearth had clearly been used for this before. Grease had soaked and cured into the surface over time, and heat ran across it in an uneven map he only partly understood. One edge caught harder where the flame licked round the side. Another held a duller steadier warmth. He understood all that half a second too late, and watched the first strips start to blacken at the edges.

"Brilliant," he muttered. "Immediately public."

He pinched one piece up, hissed when the heat caught his fingertips through the knife hilt, and dragged the worst of them back from the hottest patch. Fat spat at his wrist. Smoke curled up sharp and greasy. Behind him the room stayed mostly quiet, but he could feel curiosity replacing pure watchfulness. The stranger from the dead route was at least trying to do a normal thing.

He had been making small adjustments to the stone for a while before he noticed he was doing it — moving pieces, compensating for where the heat pooled on the left side without having decided to. Then the problem stopped being noise and arranged itself. The shine on one strip meant rendered fat. The pale bead at the corner of another meant it needed a few breaths longer. The borrowed knife had made the cuts uneven, and the uneven cuts were dictating everything after that. It was not knowledge he had earned enough to trust, only a thin extra layer of certainty dropped onto the work in front of him. The certainty was thin, but it had not belonged to him thirty seconds earlier.

The notification followed a heartbeat after.

New Skill Acquired: [Cook — Inferior]

Right. I burn it once, I learn it once. Wonderful.

I burn half the meal and that counts as progress?

It was difficult to know whether that was encouraging or deeply insulting. Possibly both. He nearly laughed, which would have been a strange thing to do over a hearth while six armed near-strangers watched him test whether dinner was safe. Instead he used the knife tip to shift the least charred pieces onto scraps of cloth and passed them round one by one, watching each face with more attention than the situation strictly deserved. The young woman by the fire took a bite, chewed, and kept chewing without incident. Under the circumstances, that seemed close enough to success. Across the hearth, the young man with the apologetic face was watching him with an expression that wasn't quite managing not to be a smile.

The young man accepted his piece, turned it once between his fingers, and lifted his brows at the char on one edge. Whatever he said had the dry sound of a joke rather than a complaint. There was a flicker at one corner of his mouth as he looked back at Adrian and spoke again, this time with obvious curiosity.

Adrian turned towards Keiran, who had taken up a place against the wall as though he had always intended to be precisely there.

"He asks if the system marked it," Keiran said.

"Yes."

The young man — Keiran supplied his name as Cael at some point during the exchange, with the offhand manner of someone providing a courtesy rather than an introduction — leaned forward at once and asked another question.

"He wants the grade."

"Inferior," Adrian said. "Which feels fair."

Something in Keiran's translation reached Cael and sat there for a moment — not pity, but something adjacent to recognition. He answered at more length, gesturing with the piece of meat in one hand as though the concept needed physical help. Keiran translated in intervals, more because the explanation came in clumps than because he seemed to be struggling.

A recognised passive was not just being vaguely good at something. The system had to see a pattern: enough repeated action under real conditions, enough adjustment, before it would start treating the behaviour as fixed. Cael demonstrated with his own grip, opening and closing his fist twice — the mime of someone who had learned a weapon passive the same way, repetition under actual use. Honest engagement. He had not expected a point system to track that as a category. Unsettling first, useful second. Inferior meant the recognition was weak, the smallest degree worth noting, but it was still recognition. Use deepened it.

Keiran translated that too. Cael nodded quickly and added another sentence, then another. His tone had warmed. Whatever reservations the room still had about Adrian, this at least was familiar territory. Someone did a task. The system noticed. There was a shape to progress.

"He says everyone starts somewhere," Keiran said. "He also says this somewhere is perhaps not impressive."

Adrian looked at the meat again. "Tell him I am relieved we agree."

That produced a laugh from Cael, unguarded enough that two others glanced over. One of them smiled as well before remembering himself. Shoulders lowered a fraction. Someone shifted closer to the fire instead of keeping as much deliberate space as before. The distance did not vanish, but it lost some of its edge. For half a minute he was not the unknown thing from the dead route.

--

Once the worst of the hunger had been dealt with, Adrian tapped two fingers to his own chest, pointed lightly round the room, then towards the door and the dark outside. He let the question sit on his face and in the angle of his shoulders. What are you doing out here?

Cael understood at once. His answer came faster and with more enthusiasm than before, enough that Keiran had to let him finish chunks of it before translating. Adrian pieced it together gradually.

The closest equivalent in his language was first ranging. A system offering for newly awakened practitioners — take a marked route into open country, deal with threats within your capability, come back alive. The point was the system's record of how you handled being useful where the land could push back. The waystation routes were monitored specifically because sealed events occasionally coincided with them; something about the patrol boundaries they followed made them attractive to whatever the system used to define event zones. Keiran mentioned this without particular emphasis, the way a person mentioned something that was known.

All of them had awakened within the last six months. None had done this particular run before.

At that point the woman in the mended coat said something short and flat, aimed at Cael by register if not by direction. He kept talking, but more carefully than before — a fraction quieter, his eyes moving briefly to Adrian as if remembering the room hadn't decided what he was yet. The woman returned to her gear without following up. It lasted only a few seconds: a reminder that warmth had a cost, and not everyone had agreed to extend it.

Adrian looked round the room again with all that in mind, and everything he had been noticing since the slope clicked harder into place.

Of course they were formal with each other. Of course the watches had been handled so carefully and the spacing on the path had looked a little too deliberate. This was not routine yet. It was the first layer of routine being laid down under observation, half by the system and half by whatever adults had sent them out expecting them to come back more solid than they had left. Even in the room he could see it now, the way decisions drifted first towards the big man or the woman in the mended coat before anyone else fully committed.

Young people on probation, then. Old enough to hold weapons, new enough that every decision still counted twice.

And I walk into the middle of it covered in pig blood and salvage. Excellent.

He asked a few more questions by gesture and expression, enough to learn that they were not a formal military unit and not quite independent either. There was backing somewhere behind them — a town, an order, a structure he could not yet name. These six were being allowed to do something real because it was time someone saw whether they could carry real work without older hands on every strap.

A veteran group might have slotted him into some existing category and moved on. These six were still deciding what counted as danger. That made them easier to understand and harder to relax around.

That should have answered the practical question. It did nothing for the larger ones. Keiran was still leaning against the wall speaking accented English in a room where nobody else understood a word Adrian said, and that fact kept scraping at the same part of his mind no matter how often survival shoved it aside.

"You still have not answered the interesting question," Adrian said quietly.

Keiran glanced at him. "There are many."

"Let's start with the one that keeps coming back. Is any of this real, and am I actually alive?"

Keiran watched him for a moment, long enough that Adrian nearly regretted asking it out loud in front of half a room of people who could not follow the words.

"Yes," Keiran said. "This is real. You are alive. And yes, this is the future."

His ribs pulled when he breathed. His hands were steady. He had gone into the cradle with a body that had spent two years losing arguments with stairs. The gamble had paid something.

The answer should have settled something. Instead it landed like a weight being moved from one shelf to another. His mother would be somewhere in that calculation — everyone he had known, gone in the direction that leaves nothing to argue with. He let it sit for as long as it was going to sit, which was not long. He had not wanted comfort. He had wanted confirmation. Unfortunately confirmation came with consequences.

"How far in the future?" Adrian asked.

"I do not know."

"You expect me to believe that."

Keiran said nothing. Adrian held the silence until it became clear Keiran would hold it longer.

"Fine," Adrian said. "Then why do you speak English? And before you say you collect things, I heard that one already."

Something moved at one corner of Keiran's mouth. "Old routes preserve old debris. Language counts."

"That is a ridiculous answer."

"It is also true."

Adrian studied him for a moment. "Am I on Earth?"

Keiran did not answer at once. His eyes went briefly to the narrow window and the dark beyond it before returning to Adrian. "You are standing in a place that inherited it."

That was worse than yes and nowhere near clean enough to be no. Honestly, the man had a gift for answers that closed less than the questions they replaced.

"Then how did a cryonics facility wake me at all? If this is really four layers of impossible piled on top of each other, why did anything in Helix still work?"

Keiran was quiet for a fraction longer than usual — the pause of something being chosen rather than retrieved. "Because something got to it before you did. The rules it was built for are gone. That does not mean everything it built has stopped."

That was more than the usual economy. Adrian noted it and did not push further.

--

By the time the meal was finished, the room had settled into the slower rhythm of people preparing to stay put. The fire was banked for endurance rather than quick heat. Bedrolls and spare cloth emerged. Water was portioned with the thoughtless care of people who already knew the numbers. Adrian was still watching all that when the healer came over and said something to Keiran.

"She wants to look at your ribs properly," he said. "A healer's examination reads registration alongside the injury assessment — standard procedure when someone comes in with damage and no prior mark noted."

Adrian peeled the coat off with less grace than he would have preferred. The healer crouched in front of him, set one hand lightly against his side, and then the warmth came.

It was more exact than ordinary heat. Something moved inward along the damage and pressed into it with quiet, deliberate purpose, finding the places that had been catching every time he breathed too deeply and smoothing the sharpest edges away. Thirty seconds later the worst of it had eased to something manageable. The last time he had expected healing to go in this direction, it hadn't. Whatever else this world turned out to be, his ribs had an opinion on whether he was alive in it.

He let out a breath and shook his head once. "That is still unsettling."

Keiran glanced at him. "Healing usually is the first time."

"Not what I meant, but fine."

The healer moved on to his knee, then the cuts on his hands. Her touch stayed brisk, professional, without the softness people sometimes put on strangers when they wanted gratitude in return. He could handle being helped. The years had made him specific about what kind.

Her hand remained on him — not healing now, but reading. Adrian felt the change mostly in the way her concentration narrowed. Her expression, which had been neutral and a little tired, tightened into focus. She looked at him once, then again, like someone checking whether a number had been copied wrongly somewhere obvious.

She said a short sentence. Keiran translated without a pause: "She says your class reads Fighter."

"I know."

The healer spoke again, longer this time, with a faint frown between her brows. Keiran was quiet for one extra beat before he translated.

"She says your Trait registration is empty."

That sentence altered the room at once.

Nobody made a scene. That almost made it worse. Cael's shoulders eased back by a fraction, as though instinct had told him to create space before his mind caught up. The archer, who had been half turned towards the fire, lifted his head and did not look away. The woman in the mended coat stopped with one buckle half-threaded through a strap. Near the door, the boy standing watch changed his footing so that he was no longer merely resting there.

The big man appeared in the low doorway at the back as if he had heard the tone more than the words. His expression had already done the arithmetic. He said two words. Keiran answered in three. This was not curiosity. This was classification.

The big man held Adrian's gaze for a second longer, apparently finished with whatever conclusion he needed, then ducked back through the doorway. He did not slam it. He did not have to.

Conversation restarted after that, but it restarted around Adrian rather than with him. Someone near the fire asked the archer a low question. The answer came back shorter. The woman in the mended coat returned to her gear, though she had turned herself just enough to keep him in the edge of her sight. Even the sounds changed — less idle overlap, more space between one voice and the next. Nobody ordered him out. Nobody drew steel. They simply became more careful about where they put attention, and none of it landed on him for free any longer.

He pulled the coat back on, slower than he wanted because his ribs objected when he reached too far. The warmth the room had briefly offered had been conditional all along. He had known that, or ought to have. Knowing it did not make the withdrawal cleaner. Then he crossed to a stretch of wall that kept him out of the walking line between hearth and door, let himself sink down against the cold stone, and stretched his bad leg a little so the knee would stop throbbing quite so hard. He sat with the fire to look at and not very much else.

Admittedly, he had treated Empty as a technical problem since the system first showed it to him — an ugly blank where the seeded second layer was supposed to sit. Annoying. Probably relevant. He had not pushed on it, partly because drawing attention to something that might mark him as anomalous seemed worse than not understanding it, and partly because survival had kept shunting the question to a later that kept not arriving.

In this room it stopped being technical. It was the thing that made a laugh vanish, made the big man do quick sums in the doorway, and left everyone else's attention on him a beat longer than it wanted to. These people could read a passive grade from a half-burned meal and send youngsters out on a monitored first ranging. The moment they heard Trait registration is empty, the room changed. To them it was something they already had a word for.

In fact, the logic ran further than that. Trait was the seeded second layer — the thing that narrowed class into something with a direction. Without it, later growth had no inherited direction, nothing to weight what he became toward any predictable shape. No, that was not exactly the right frame either: not unmoored, since unmoored implied a direction that had been lost. More like a build with no foundation spec. A Fighter's class, one cooking passive at Inferior, and everything else blank.

And the blank did not stop there. Keiran had confirmed this was real, he was alive, this was the future. The simulation theory had less to recommend it than it had an hour ago. That was not the same as feeling better about the alternative. If Helix had only worked because something later had reached back and changed it, who had done that, and why had the system grabbed him now? He had survived cryostasis, missed whatever had happened to the world, and woken at the exact moment a dead route decided to open beneath a hill. That was coincidence in the technical sense. It was just getting harder to believe in coincidence as a governing principle.

The fire burned lower until one of the others leaned in to feed it another length of wood, and the new flame threw the room into sharper movement for a second before settling again. Across from him Cael glanced his way, got as far as opening his mouth, then thought better of it and looked down at his hands instead. Adrian understood the decision even while it irritated him.

He rested his head briefly against the stone behind him and tried to decide what the immediate consequence actually was. They still needed to sleep somewhere. Throwing him outside injured would have been wasteful as well as cruel, and nothing in their behaviour so far suggested pointless cruelty. More likely the cost would be distance, caution, and the assumption that any help offered to him ought to stay limited until someone wiser decided what an Empty stranger was worth risking. Not ideal, but not a tonight problem unless somebody made it one.

He was still turning that over when fresh text slid into his vision.

[System Event Pending]

[The Hunt]

Adrian read the title once. Titles were either literal or worse than literal, and he had no good reason to assume this one was going to surprise him. He opened it.

A predator event has been initiated within the sealed boundary. All registered practitioners within the boundary are active participants. Removal from the designated zone is restricted until the event concludes or all participants are eliminated. Primary objective: survival. Secondary objectives available upon engagement. Event duration: 30 days. Event begins in: 12 hours.

The reward field listed items, credits, and system terms he did not yet have enough context to value. Sealed boundary and departure restricted were bad enough, active participants made it worse, and thirty days made it genuinely absurd in a way that did not improve on re-reading. Every clause remained exactly as bad as it had first looked. If the system meant what it said, then this first ranging had just stopped being a monitored exercise and turned into a sealed event with survival as the opening condition. That sounded less like apprenticeship and more like being informed by bureaucracy that a disaster had already been filed.

He looked up. The change in the room confirmed he had not misunderstood by very much.

The group had gone the kind of quiet that came from processing something without enough time for denial to do useful work. Near the door, one of the others swore under his breath. The big man came back through the low doorway at once, nearly catching one shoulder on the frame because he had moved too fast to duck properly. He spoke to the healer first, sharp and low. She answered without any softness in it.

Keiran had not moved much. Which was exactly why Adrian watched him harder than the others.

Up to now Keiran had carried himself with that same infuriating ease he had brought down the hill, as if every development was either expected or at least insufficiently surprising to bother him. That had altered. Not by much. A fraction around the eyes. Less looseness in the mouth. His attention had gone inward and outward at once, the way someone's did when they were mapping implications faster than they wanted to show.

It lasted only a few seconds before he schooled it back into place, but Adrian had already seen enough. If even Keiran was giving this prompt his full attention, then the event was either rare, badly timed, or wrong in some more specific way Adrian lacked the vocabulary to name.

"How bad is that?" Adrian asked quietly.

Keiran kept his eyes on the room for a moment longer before answering. "Bad enough that the next twelve hours matter."

"What do you know about the boundary size?"

Keiran did not answer immediately.

"Keiran."

"Larger than this route," he said. "Smaller than the settlement to the east."

Adrian shifted his gaze to the man in the corner. "You do know that I have absolutely no idea where the settlement to the east is, nor do I have any idea what a route, or any of it means."

"In this case let me clarify this for you. This will be your first real challenge in your presumably last life."

The fire cracked softly in the middle of the silence, absurdly normal. Cael was looking at his own hands. Thirty days. He did not think about what that meant yet.

How the fuck am I supposed to survive thirty days in the wild, surrounded by monsters, trying to kill and probably eat me?
