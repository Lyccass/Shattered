## CATEGORY 1: STRUCTURAL TELL THRESHOLDS (AGENTS.md)

### FLAG 1.1 — Negation-reveal constructions (at threshold / over)

**Rule:** Negation-reveal constructions such as "Not X. Not Y. The real thing." — max 2 per chapter. A third use is a revision target.

**Instance A:**
> "That got through to him more than the bodies had. Not the killing, exactly, or even the part where one of the things had nearly gotten into his ribs."

Classic soft negation-reveal: "Not the killing" dismisses the surface reading and primes for the real thing, which follows immediately. The construction is present and doing its job.

**Instance B:**
> "Not because the sword was bad. Because it assumed a man who knew what he was doing."

Explicit two-part negation-reveal. Grammatically clean example of the banned construction. Lands well, but is the second use.

**Instance C:**
> "He was still clumsy, still late on the recovery, still one poor step away from doing something terminally stupid, but the movement at least felt understandable."

This is softer — not a grammatical negation structure but a rhetorical "three negatives before the pivot" construction using "still...still...still...but." It performs the same function: three dismissed negatives before the reveal of the real state. It qualifies as the third use of this rhetorical shape in the chapter.

**Correction for Instance C:** Collapse the triple "still" stack. The movement being understandable is the point; the three "still" negations are setup. Pick the most specific one (the recovery timing) and put the understandable movement on equal footing with it rather than after the rhetorical climb. Example: "He was still late on the recovery and one bad step from disaster, but the movement felt understandable in a way the pipe never had."

### FLAG 1.2 — One-word paragraph landing beats (over threshold)

**Rule:** One-word paragraph landing beats such as "Real." or "Good." — max 2 per chapter. A third is a revision target.

**Instance A:**
> "Now we're talking."

Short isolated line following the system prompt reveal. Functions as a one-to-three word landing beat that closes the token-focus moment.

**Instance B:**
> "Thank fuck," he whispered.

Isolated paragraph, one short phrase, functioning as a beat landing on the water sequence. Earns its isolation — it is the only genuine release in a tense stretch.

**Instance C:**
> "Ugly work," he murmured. "That I can do."

Isolated two-sentence paragraph functioning as a landing beat on the spear-focus moment. The second sentence in particular ("That I can do") is performing the same structural role as a one-word closer: it is the spoken equivalent of "Good." It lands the beat, closes the moment, and then the chapter continues.

Three isolated short-line landings across a single chapter. "Now we're talking" (Instance A) is the least load-bearing of the three and is the revision target.

**Correction for Instance A:** Fold the reaction into the surrounding narration rather than isolating it. The satisfaction of the token resolving can be shown in what Adrian does immediately after, or in the narration's rhythm shifting, rather than a quoted line standing alone as a beat.

### FLAG 1.3 — Two-line contrast or pivot stacks (over threshold)

**Rule:** Two-line contrast or pivot stacks — max 2 clear cases per chapter. A third is a revision target.

**Instance A:**
> "He looked down at his hands, then at his left leg, then back at his hands because one check clearly was not going to satisfy whatever frantic little inspector had taken over his nervous system."

This is a single sentence — not a stack. Not flagged.

**Instance B:**
> "Not because the sword was bad. Because it assumed a man who knew what he was doing."

Two-line pivot stack. First line dismisses. Second delivers.

**Instance C:**
> "The proof was still there. The ache in his forearm was real, but the grip underneath it felt denser somehow, less near its limit than it should have after a fight, a panic spiral, and blood loss."

Two-unit stack: short landing sentence followed by qualifying expansion. The short opener is performing a beat-landing role before the explanation unfolds. This is the construction used when a writer wants a punchy declarative but also needs to develop the thought.

**Instance D:**
> "That was not training. He had not secretly become competent in the last thirty seconds. The system had just reached in and made movement easier."

Three-sentence stack where the first two sentences are negation and the third delivers the meaning. This is both a negation-reveal (see Flag 1.1) and a pivot stack. The first two sentences are performing pure setup for the third. A three-line pivot stack of this structure is a revision target.

**Correction for Instance D:** This is the most formally complete version of the pattern in the chapter. Collapse the first two sentences. The point is the third: "The system had just reached in and made movement easier" — that sentence contains everything. The two negations before it are scaffolding. Example: "He had not learned anything in the last thirty seconds. The system had simply made movement easier, and that was its own unsettling thing."

### FLAG 1.4 — Parallel stacking (approaching threshold)

**Rule:** Parallel stacking and tri-line blocks must be treated as rare emphasis devices. More than three clearly similar vertical stacks in one chapter is a revision target.

**Instance A (the internal spiral):**
> "*What the fuck was happening?* *How long had he been here?* *Why were there monsters in the walls of a dead cryonics facility?* *Why was any of this real?*"

Four parallel italicised questions in sequence. This is the highest-density parallel stack in the chapter and it earns its format — these are involuntary panic thoughts, the content justifies the percussion, and this is inside a pressure sequence. It operates within the spirit of the rule even at four items.

**Instance B (wound inventory):**
> "Three claw lines crossed his left ribs, shallow but open. Scratches along the collarbone had bled theatrically, and his right knee was already shading into a bruise that promised tomorrow would be educational in all the wrong ways."

Three-item parallel list of wounds. Functional, specific, within threshold as incidental description.

**Instance C (the cache weapons):**
> "There was a sword in a plain scabbard, a hatchet, a hammer, a compact shield, bandages, a flat tin of sharply herbal salve, a second water bladder, and two wrapped long shafts tucked underneath the lot."

Eight-item inventory list. This is a deliberate catalogue, appropriate to the scene. The rule targets tri-line stacks used for rhetorical weight, not honest inventories. Not flagged.

**Instance D (the weapons assessment sequence):**
The sword, hatchet, hammer, and shield each receive their own paragraph following a nearly identical structural pattern: Adrian picks it up, feels something promising, tests it, rejects it, gives the reason. Four consecutive paragraphs sharing the same shape is a parallel stack at the paragraph level — each paragraph is a slight variation on "promising / but / rejected because." This repetition is partially justified by the content (testing weapons is genuinely repetitive) but the structural mirroring is exact enough across four iterations to be a tell. By the fourth item (the shield) the pattern is fully predictable.

**Correction for Instance D:** The sword, hatchet, and hammer can stay close to the current structure. The shield paragraph is the fourth beat in the pattern and the reader already knows the conclusion before arriving at the sentence. Either cut the shield's reasoning shorter (Adrian picks it up, immediately knows it is wrong, dismisses it in one sentence) or change the paragraph's form — arrive at the rejection through a different angle so the fourth item does not simply echo the first three.

## CATEGORY 2: ANTI-AI TELL LOCK (AGENTS.md)

### FLAG 2.1 — Polished emotional landing lines (exceeding threshold)

**Rule:** Do not keep writing polished emotional landing lines just because they sound good.

The chapter produces a new landing line at the close of nearly every major section:

- "The relief came off that realisation so hard it nearly folded him."
- "That did not solve anything. It did not get him water. It did not get him out. It did not stop whatever lived in the walls from coming back."
- "Somebody here had panicked intelligently, which was oddly comforting."
- "Normal had left hours ago. Maybe centuries ago."
- "Fine. He could hate that later."
- "Getting out did not start with sprinting blindly into the corridor while shaking hard enough to miss the door."
- "The pipe had already taught him the underlying lesson. Reach bought time. The spear was that lesson refined by someone who had known what they were doing."

Seven clear instances of a paragraph or unit closed by a deliberately weighted, composed landing line. Several of these are genuinely good sentences. The problem is the frequency: every major section of the chapter — emotional aftermath, gear assessment, stat reasoning, weapon selection — ends by resolving into a polished summation. The prose stops feeling discovered and starts feeling assembled.

**Correction targets:** "Somebody here had panicked intelligently, which was oddly comforting" is the most author-facing of these — it is the narrator summarising the meaning of a detail rather than Adrian processing it. "Normal had left hours ago. Maybe centuries ago." is the most constructed (and is also a two-beat pivot stack; see Flag 1.3). "Fine. He could hate that later." has become a structural habit across multiple chapters and should be varied or cut here. Revise at minimum two of the seven toward endings that transition rather than close.

### FLAG 2.2 — Symmetrical architecture: repeated emotional beat pattern

**Rule:** Avoid too much symmetry, compositional neatness, or over-deliberate callback architecture.

The chapter follows a consistent emotional rhythm across its weapon-testing and stat-reasoning sequences: Adrian encounters something → assesses it → finds it insufficient → dismisses it → moves on to the next thing. This pattern runs cleanly through: maintenance pipe (established in chapter) → stats assessed → sword → hatchet → hammer → shield → spear (success). The sequence is fully resolved with the spear being exactly right. Every item encountered is evaluated and disposed of in the correct order, building to the correct conclusion.

The architecture is too neat. The items form a perfect escalating sequence toward the predetermined answer. In particular, the shield paragraph ("The shield annoyed him most because he wanted it to work") tips the hand: the narration acknowledges that the character wants it to work, then provides a perfectly articulated three-sentence reason why it does not, and moves on. The composure of the rejection is too complete.

This is a mild flag. The sequence is functional and the spear is the right answer. The problem is that the journey to the answer feels managed rather than lived. One item should surprise Adrian, resist easy categorisation, or require him to revisit the decision rather than dispose of it in one organised paragraph.

### FLAG 2.3 — Repeated sentence skeleton: "It did not X. It did not Y."

**Rule:** Do not default to repeated sentence skeletons across a chapter.

**Instance A:**
> "That did not solve anything. It did not get him water. It did not get him out. It did not stop whatever lived in the walls from coming back."

Three consecutive "It did not X" sentences.

**Instance B:**
> "Not painless. Not graceful. Just stronger."

Three-item negation sequence resolving to the positive. Same skeleton, different grammatical length.

**Instance C:**
> "He had been slow because he was scared, clumsy because he was untrained, and nearly dead because he had gone monster-hunting with a length of pipe in a cupboard-sized room. He had not been slow because his own body had betrayed him."

The "He had been X / He had not been X" inversion is the same skeleton at sentence level.

Three instances of the negation-then-assertion skeleton in a single chapter. Instance A is the most structurally dominant because it runs three consecutive parallel sentences. Instance B is structurally identical to the "Not X. Not Y. Just Z." pattern (see Flag 1.1). These accumulate into a recognisable rhythmic habit.

**Correction:** Instance A is the primary revision target — collapse the three parallel "It did not" sentences. The point is that the spiral does not help him practically. That can be made in one sentence, and the three-beat setup over-signals the conclusion.

## CATEGORY 3: THOUGHT PATTERN LOCK / INNER THOUGHT RULES (AGENTS.md)

### FLAG 3.1 — Inner thought count (at or over threshold)

**Rule:** Use two to three instances per chapter across both types of inner thought combined. Overuse kills the punch.

Inner thought instances in the chapter (italicised, formatted as direct inner voice):

1. *The numbers had done that.*
2. *What the fuck was happening? How long had he been here? Why were there monsters in the walls of a dead cryonics facility? Why was any of this real?*
3. *Nope.*
4. *Too close.*

Four instances. The rule allows two to three. The chapter is one over threshold.

**Assessment by instance:**
- Instance 1 (*The numbers had done that.*) — Type 1 involuntary flash. Earns its place. Keep.
- Instance 2 (the four panic questions) — Type 2 reasoning monologue, here expressed as interrogatives. Earns its place in the panic sequence. Keep.
- Instance 3 (*Nope.*) — Type 1 involuntary flash. Single-word. Earns its place as the self-correction against hysteria. Keep.
- Instance 4 (*Too close.*) — Type 1 involuntary flash. The weakest of the four. It names the thing that already been shown by "the bay seemed to shrink around him again" and the return to spear-readiness. The narration has already done this work.

**Correction:** Cut Instance 4 (*Too close.*). The narration surrounding it — the bay shrinking, the involuntary reach for the spear — already performs this function. The italicised flash adds a named label to a physical reaction that has already been rendered, which reverses the rule's intent (thought should interrupt or add; it should not restate what the body just demonstrated).

### FLAG 3.2 — Inner thought during or adjacent to combat action

**Rule:** No inner thoughts during direct combat action.

**Instance:** The four-question panic sequence (*What the fuck was happening?...*) occurs in the aftermath section immediately following the fight, not during it. This is compliant.

**Instance 1** (*The numbers had done that.*) occurs at the moment of levelling up, which technically coincides with the final act of the fight — the last twitching creature receiving the killing blow. The thought appears *before* Adrian drives the pipe in, triggered by the level-up sensation.

Reading the sequence carefully: the italicised thought lands, then "Then the last one twitched" — meaning the thought appeared while the fight had a live enemy still present. This is borderline. The rule bans inner thoughts "during direct combat action." Adrian is not mid-swing when the thought occurs; he has paused after the level-up sensation hits. However, the enemy has not yet been confirmed dead. The thought slightly dilutes the combat-action zone.

**Correction (optional):** Move the italicised thought to after the confirmation strike — after "The thing made a wet scraping noise against the metal plates and then, finally, stayed still" — so it lands as genuine aftermath rather than mid-action. The beat would be stronger there anyway.

## CATEGORY 4: HARD BANS (AGENTS.md)

### FLAG 4.1 — Exposition with no present-scene trigger (borderline)

**Rule (Hard Bans):** No exposition with no present-scene trigger.

**Instance:**
> "Somebody here had panicked intelligently, which was oddly comforting. Water sat right at the front..."

The phrase "People made caches when they had time, fear, access to tools, and very little interest in dying empty-handed" is a generalised statement of observed human behaviour used to foreshadow the cache contents. It is not strictly triggered by something Adrian sees or touches in the immediate scene — it arrives before he opens the cache, as a rationalised expectation. The trigger is the seam on the panel, which Adrian observed before the fight. The exposition is therefore partially delayed from its trigger rather than absent one.

This is a mild flag, not a hard violation. The explanation of cache-making behaviour is one step removed from observation into general principle, which edges toward exposition-without-trigger. The narration could stay tighter by having the generalised principle emerge from what Adrian actually sees when the panel opens rather than arriving in anticipation.

**Correction:** Move "People made caches when they had time, fear, access to tools, and very little interest in dying empty-handed" to after the panel opens and Adrian sees the organised compartment. The visible organisation is the trigger; the principle should follow it, not precede it.

## OVERALL COMPLIANCE SCORE: 7 / 10

The chapter holds the voice well throughout. The protagonist's analytical sequence is reliable, the diction is consistent, the system-text handling is clean and functional, British English habits are natural rather than forced, and the physical/emotional processing is rendered through behaviour rather than declaration in most places. The scene has genuine forward pressure at its close.

The dominant failure pattern is **structural over-reliability**. The chapter reaches for the same set of landing devices too frequently: negation-reveal constructions appear three times, polished closing lines end every major section, one-word and short-phrase beat isolates accumulate to three, and the weapons-assessment sequence repeats its structural shape four times in a row without variation. None of these devices is wrong in isolation; the problem is the regularity with which they appear. A reader attending to rhythm rather than content will feel the chapter assembling itself toward its conclusions rather than discovering them. The Anti-AI Tell Lock exists to catch exactly this: the tells are not lexical but architectural — the same scaffolding underneath too many different rooms.

Priority revision targets, in order: (1) cut at minimum two of the seven polished landing lines identified in Flag 2.1, particularly "Somebody here had panicked intelligently" and "Normal had left hours ago"; (2) collapse the three-sentence pivot stack in Flag 1.3 Instance D ("That was not training..."); (3) cut the fourth inner thought instance (*Too close.*) per Flag 3.1; (4) break the structural repetition in the weapons-assessment sequence by shortening the shield paragraph per Flag 1.4 Instance D; (5) collapse the triple "It did not" skeleton in Flag 2.3 Instance A.
