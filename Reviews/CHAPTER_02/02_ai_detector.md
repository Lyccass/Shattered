## Structural Tells

### Rule of Three — multiple hits

**Hit 1:**
> "He did not want clever. He wanted a body that answered, strength he could spend directly, and a class description that did not quietly assume he already knew a second language made of system terms."

Three-item list (body that answered / strength he could spend directly / a class description that...) used to complete the reasoning rather than for dramatic necessity. The third item is padding — the thought is complete at "strength he could spend directly." Rewrite: "He wanted a body that answered and strength he could spend without reading a manual first."

**Hit 2:**
> "Preservation under pressure. Attrition. Recovery. Group endurance."

Four-word fragment stacks used as a beat to dramatise Adrian's rejection of Healer. Reads as list-making for rhetorical effect. Two items carry the dismissal; four items are completeness. Rewrite: "Preservation under pressure. Group endurance." — the middle two items add nothing the surrounding paragraph hasn't already said.

**Hit 3:**
> "Not a body in any useful present-tense sense, just straps, grey residue, and a shape that had once relied on bones."

The three-item list (straps / grey residue / a shape) reads as a completeness grouping. The third item is the one that does the work. Rewrite: "Not a body in any useful present-tense sense — just a shape that had once relied on bones."

---

### Negative Parallelism — one hit

> "He did not want clever. He wanted a body that answered..."

Mild X/not-X pivot. Single instance, does not reach threshold, but combined with the three-rule-of-three hits above, it contributes to a pattern of parallelism-for-structure.

---

### Thesis-Antithesis-Synthesis Reasoning — one hit

The four-class evaluation sequence is the clearest structural tell in the chapter. Adrian evaluates each class, critiques or steelmans it, and arrives at Fighter through a process of elimination that is too clean and too evenly weighted:

- Fighter: *"That, at least, sounded honest."* (provisional approval)
- Ranger: *"it fit... which was exactly why he distrusted it a little."* (steelman then rebuttal)
- Caster: reasoning about unknown rules, concludes it's a *"terrible place to start"* (logical rejection)
- Healer: *"That felt almost insulting."* (emotional rejection)
- Return to Fighter: *"blunt enough to be almost stupid, which was exactly what he liked about it"* (character-based confirmation)

Each class gets roughly the same word count, the same logic arc, and the same dismissal mechanism. The structure is thesis (each class has merit) — antithesis (each has a disqualifying flaw) — synthesis (Fighter wins). It is too evenly balanced to feel like a mind in motion. A human writer would have one class take more space than the others, one dismissal be cruder or faster, and the conclusion arrived at with more friction.

**Rewrite direction:** The Ranger section is the emotionally loaded one — it should be longer and messier than the others. Caster and Healer can be much shorter, barely considered. The imbalance would feel more honest.

## Formatting and Rhythm Tells

### Over-Symmetrical Paragraph Endings — one hit

The class-evaluation sequence ends each class block on a summary-dismissal sentence:

- Fighter block ends: *"Balanced for survival in contested environments was either reassuring or a polite way of saying this was the choice for people expected to get into ugly situations quickly."*
- Ranger block ends: *"All sensible. Maybe too sensible."*
- Caster block ends: *"...higher precision with strong growth through system literacy sounded an awful lot like a path for people who already knew where the floor was."*
- Healer block ends: *"...boiled down to hold everything together and help everybody else last longer."*

Four consecutive blocks each closing on a summary-dismissal statement. The symmetry is mechanical rather than expressive. A container has been built and each class fitted into it identically.

### Parenthetical Explanation for Reader's Benefit — one hit

> "Whoever built it had expected maintenance from the inside while it was active. You only specified that when shutting the thing down would have been catastrophically inconvenient."

The second sentence exists to explain the implication of the first for the reader's benefit. Adrian is alone — he is not explaining to anyone. The inference is dressed as Adrian's thought but functions as authorial annotation. A human writer would let the image stand or fold the implication into the observation itself: "Inspection hatches sized for a person to enter while the thing was running — whoever built this had never planned to shut it down easily."

## Narrative Red Flags

### Telling Emotions with Clinical Precision — two hits

**Hit 1:**
> "For a second he very nearly laughed. For another second he very nearly cried, which was worse, so he got on with the more immediate problem of being trapped in a box."

The emotion is labelled ("very nearly cried") and immediately contextualised ("which was worse"). The labelling is clinical. The parenthetical self-assessment ("which was worse") is an author's gloss. A more human rendering would give the physical sensation without naming it, and trust the pivot to action to carry the suppression. Rewrite direction: drop "which was worse" — the pivot to "more immediate problem" already tells us he shut it down.

**Hit 2:**
> "That one tightened something in his chest for a different reason."

"For a different reason" is a tag the author adds to distinguish this emotional response categorically from the previous one. It reads as a clarifying label rather than a sensation. A human writer would render the difference through what the tightening prompts him to do, not what it is labelled as. Rewrite: cut "for a different reason" — the content of the Ranger passage makes the distinction clear without naming it.

### Protagonist Growth Signalled by Explicit Statement — one hit

> "His body just worked. Not gracefully, not with any sudden hidden athlete waiting underneath, but it worked."

The physical recovery is shown through action (bending, crouching, rising), but then immediately stated and pre-emptively qualified. "Not gracefully, not with any sudden hidden athlete waiting underneath" is a defensive clarification for the reader. A human writer trusts the crouching and rising. Rewrite: cut the qualification. The action shows it; the statement undercuts it.

### Repeated Structural Mirroring — one hit

The chapter contains two mechanically similar evaluation sequences: Adrian testing his body after waking (bend knee, shift weight, crouch, rise, touch forearm, touch thigh), and Adrian evaluating the class options (read description, interpret terms, weigh against situation, reject or accept). Both follow identical internal logic: methodical enumeration, evaluation, conclusion. The mirroring is not thematic — it is the same cognitive subroutine running twice on different inputs. One of the two sequences should be structurally dissimilar from the other.

---

## Naturalness Score: 7/10

The chapter is well above the floor — the vocabulary is clean, the dialogue is natural, and the protagonist's observational voice is genuinely distinctive. What keeps it from scoring higher is the structural regularity beneath the surface: the four-class evaluation is too evenly balanced, the rule-of-three appears more than once without necessity, and the emotional labelling in the opening section is too clinical for a character who processes feeling through behaviour rather than through statement. A human writer at this level would have rougher edges in the architecture.
