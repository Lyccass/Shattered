## 1. Sentence Rhythm

**FLAG 1 — Short-sentence run outside pressure exchange**

> "The voices came over the slope thirty seconds later."

This sentence concludes a paragraph that begins after the standoff decision has been made — tension is suspended, not active. The four sentences immediately preceding it are all short (12 words or fewer):

> "He wiped his hands on his coat, which was already ruined. Straightened as much as his ribs allowed. Tucked what remained of the knife into his pocket so it was not visible at first glance."

"Straightened as much as his ribs allowed" — 9 words. "Tucked what remained of the knife into his pocket so it was not visible at first glance" — 17 words, acceptable. But "He wiped his hands on his coat, which was already ruined" — 12 words, borderline — followed immediately by the 9-word fragment "Straightened as much as his ribs allowed" with no explicit subject (it reads as a bare fragment, not a full sentence). The clipped rhythm here is not combat percussion; the boar is dead, the group has not yet appeared. The rule from PROSE_ANALYSIS.md requires short sentences as "deliberate punctuation," and flags "any stretch of three or more consecutive sentences that are all short (under 10 words) outside a pressure exchange." The bare fragment "Straightened as much as his ribs allowed" functions as a sub-10-word unit without a subject, making this the third short unit in a row.

**Correction:** Merge at least two of these micro-actions into a single medium sentence so the rhythm is not entirely clipped during a non-combat transitional moment. Example: "He wiped his hands on his coat — already ruined — straightened as much as his ribs allowed, and tucked what remained of the knife into his pocket."

## 2. Paragraph Shape

**FLAG 2 — Post-pressure clipped rhythm continues unchanged**

After the boar dies, the prose enters its aftermath section. PROSE_ANALYSIS.md states: "After a pressure peak, paragraphs lengthen again. The aftermath breathes differently from the fight." AGENTS.md Anti-AI Tell Lock: "After pressure scenes, widen back out. The aftermath should breathe differently from the peak tension."

The two paragraphs immediately following the boar's death:

> "For half a second Adrian just stayed there with the broken knife clenched in one hand and the other buried in blood-wet bristles, not trusting the stillness enough to believe it. Then a rush of heat hit beneath his sternum."

> "It came hard and deep, like something hot had been driven straight through the centre of him and then pulled wide. Adrian sucked in a sharp breath and nearly choked on it. Every muscle in his body tightened at once. The rush was worse than adrenaline and stranger than pain, a violent pulse that flooded through his limbs and made his hands shake even harder than they already had."

These are acceptably medium-length. But the paragraphs that follow — the carcass removal, the voices, the decision — do not widen. They stay in 2–3 sentence units through the entirety of the post-combat sequence. The rhythm does not lengthen or open up; it remains in the same choppy register as the late-combat section. Compare: the dialogue section with Keiran finally opens into longer paragraphs, but that is five major beats after the pressure has resolved.

**Correction:** In the immediate post-combat block (from "That was when the sound reached him" onward), push at least two paragraphs to 4–5 sentences with longer analytical sentences, allowing the prose to breathe before the group arrives.

**FLAG 3 — Micro-paragraph percussion outside combat**

The following sequence appears during the standoff — tension is present but this is not active combat:

> "Nobody moved."

> "He raised both hands slowly to shoulder height."

> "She spoke again. A different phrasing, still nothing."

> "He gestured at himself, then at the boar, then spread both hands again."

> "He tried to make it look less ridiculous. He failed."

> "The man with the arrow kept his aim steady."

That is six consecutive single-sentence (or functionally single-sentence) paragraphs outside active combat. AGENTS.md: "More than four consecutive single-sentence paragraphs outside a pressure exchange is a revision target." This run has at minimum five to six qualifying units.

**Correction:** Consolidate at minimum two adjacent beats into a paragraph with 3–4 sentences. The mime sequence ("He gestured at himself... He failed") could absorb "She spoke again. A different phrasing, still nothing" and the arrow-holder's attention into a single paragraph that carries all four observations without separate breaks.

## 4. Structural Tell Thresholds

**FLAG 4 — Negation-reveal construction: third instance**

The chapter contains the following negation-reveal constructions:

Instance 1:
> "Running was not viable. His knee had opinions about running. His ribs had louder ones."

This is not a strict Not X / Not Y / Real thing construction, but it is in the negation-reveal family.

Instance 2 (clear):
> "He did not have the strength to overpower it, only enough to keep pressing into the damage already there and hope the body beneath him gave out first."

Single sentence — not a stacked structure.

Instance 3 (clear):
> "It was either an honest answer or a very well-shaped deflection. Adrian could not yet tell which."

Not a negation-reveal.

Let me re-examine for the explicit pattern. The cleanest instance:

> "He was not performing calm. He simply appeared to have a different relationship with the situation than everyone else present."

This is a two-sentence negation-reveal: negative statement → restatement of what is actually true. This is one instance.

> "It was not an unfriendly look."

Single-sentence negation, not the stacked structure.

> "Not a charge. A walk."

Two-word negation-reveal. This is a second instance.

> "Not aggressive exactly, but not encouraging either."

Embedded negation — borderline.

At strict count: "Not a charge. A walk." and "He was not performing calm. He simply appeared to have a different relationship..." are both qualifying negation-reveals. The chapter does not exceed two clear instances of the full stacked pattern. **No flag needed on count alone.**

However, the general density of negation-framing across the chapter is high. This is noted but not a threshold violation.

**FLAG 4 (reassigned) — Two-line contrast stacks: exceeding two instances**

AGENTS.md: "Two-line contrast or pivot stacks must be treated as rare. If the pair can be one sentence without loss, it should be one sentence. More than two clear cases in one chapter is a revision target."

Instance 1:
> "He was not performing calm. He simply appeared to have a different relationship with the situation than everyone else present."

These two sentences are one thought and can be one sentence: "He was not performing calm — he simply appeared to have a different relationship with the situation than everyone else."

Instance 2:
> "Not a charge. A walk."

One sentence: "It was not a charge but a walk."

Instance 3:
> "It was either an honest answer or a very well-shaped deflection. Adrian could not yet tell which."

One sentence: "It was either an honest answer or a very well-shaped deflection — he could not yet tell which."

Instance 4:
> "He did not know this man. He did not know this land, this group, or what waited below."

One sentence with no loss.

Four instances exceeds the threshold of two. **This is a revision target.**

**Correction:** Collapse instances 1, 2, 3, and 4 as shown above. Keep no more than two such pairs in the final draft.

**FLAG 5 — Rule of three: list assembled for completeness**

> "The dead beast still crushed him into the hillside, its weight across his ribs, hip, and one trapped leg, like a collapsed wall of meat and scale..."

Ribs, hip, and trapped leg — three body locations. The third item (trapped leg) is the mechanically relevant one (it's what he's trying to free). Two items, or restructuring to "its weight across his chest and one trapped leg," removes the completeness padding.

Source: AGENTS.md — "Do not default to the rule of three. Lists should have the number of items the thought actually requires, not the number that sounds polished."

**Correction:** "its weight still across his chest and one trapped leg, like a collapsed wall of meat and scale."

**FLAG 6 — Rule of three: second instance**

> "caution, suspicion, and the kind of attention that went with someone who needed information more than they needed a fight."

Three-item emotional read. The third item is the most specific and is doing real work. The first two (caution and suspicion) are near-synonymous in context; the list is padding the third. Two items: "suspicion and the particular attention of someone who needed information more than a fight."

Source: AGENTS.md — "Lists should have the number of items the thought actually requires."

**Correction:** "He watched her face instead and got suspicion and the particular attention of someone who needed information more than they needed a fight."

## 5. POV Lock

**FLAG 7 — Neutral narrator intrusion**

> "The man coming down from the higher ground moved like the land owed him nothing. Medium height, lean in a way that suggested purpose rather than shortage, wearing gear that struck Adrian immediately as slightly too clean for the frontier without being anything he could put his finger on precisely. His hands were loose at his sides. He was not performing calm. He simply appeared to have a different relationship with the situation than everyone else present."

The final two sentences — "He was not performing calm. He simply appeared to have a different relationship with the situation than everyone else present." — read as a neutral narrator's assessment of Keiran's psychology. Adrian cannot know whether Keiran is "performing" calm or possessing it genuinely; this is an interior-state claim about a stranger he is meeting for the first time. The correct register is Adrian's *read* of surface evidence, not a conclusion about Keiran's inner state.

Source: AGENTS.md POV Lock — "The narration must only include what he sees, infers, remembers, identifies, suspects, or decides." Free indirect style rule — "even neutral-looking narration should still feel colored by his priorities and phrasing."

**Correction:** Filter through Adrian's observation rather than rendering Keiran's inner state: "His hands were loose at his sides, and nothing in his face or posture was doing the work of looking relaxed — he simply looked like a man to whom this particular situation did not register as a problem."

## 6. Protagonist Thought Pattern

**FLAG 8 — Inner thought during active combat (Type 1)**

> *No. No, I am not dying to a mutant pig on a hillside.*

Source: AGENTS.md — "No inner thoughts during direct combat action. The fight carries itself through narration and physical description. Inner thoughts belong to: pre-combat planning, post-combat emotional processing, system-analysis moments, or emotionally significant discoveries."

This thought occurs while the boar is actively on top of Adrian and has just nearly gored him with a tusk. This is mid-combat, not pre-combat planning. The thought functions as motivation delivery that the narration should be carrying through action and sensation.

**Correction:** Remove the formatted thought. Convert the emotional content to behavioral narration: "Adrian jammed his elbow in harder, not thinking about dying — just about the next thing, the thing after that." Or simply let the action carry it and cut the thought entirely.

**FLAG 9 — Inner thought during active combat (Type 2)**

> *If it gets those legs under it properly, this is over. There is no clever recovery after that. There is just me bleeding out in the grass because I got flattened by an armoured pig.*

Source: AGENTS.md — same rule as above. This thought occurs during the fight, while Adrian is physically pinned and the boar is actively struggling. This is direct combat action, not a pause for reasoning.

Additionally, this is a Type 2 thought (first-person reasoning monologue) and it reads as a conclusion already reached rather than a mind in motion. AGENTS.md: "Show reasoning in motion, not conclusions. A thought that begins with the problem and works toward a redirect is correct. A thought that simply states the conclusion is narration that forgot to be narration."

**Correction:** Remove the formatted thought block. The tactical assessment ("if it gets those legs under it, this is over") is good information but belongs in behavioral narration during a brief action lull, or must be cut entirely if there is no lull. The self-deprecating "armoured pig" observation could survive as a half-second flash after the fact.

**FLAG 10 — Inner thought during active combat (Type 1)**

> *Just die. Please just die before I run out of strength first.*

Source: AGENTS.md — same rule. This thought appears while Adrian is still actively stabbing the boar, in the middle of the fight. The fight has not resolved. This is direct combat action.

Additionally, AGENTS.md states: "Do not use inner thoughts to substitute for narration that should carry physical or emotional state. Adrenaline, fear, and body-shock belong in behavioral narration." This thought is carrying desperation that should be visible in the physical description — the shaking arms, the failing grip, the blood on his hands.

**Correction:** Cut the thought. The paragraph surrounding it already carries the physical exhaustion effectively. The desperation does not need to be named; it is present in "he did not have the strength to overpower it, only enough to keep pressing."

## 7. Diction

**FLAG 11 — Absent self-correction move across extended reasoning passage**

The internal reasoning block about whether to run or stay is one of the chapter's longest analytical passages:

> "Running was not viable. His knee had opinions about running. His ribs had louder ones. The slope would turn a serious sprint into a controlled tumble before he covered fifty yards, and even if he managed to stay upright, six people who knew this land would close the distance before he found better ground. He was also coated in blood from wrist to chin and standing directly over the carcass of an armoured pig the size of a small car. Running from that did not look innocent. It looked like something a guilty man did."

This passage states conclusions confidently and never self-corrects or refines a claim. The voice is composed and declarative throughout. Compare PROSE_ANALYSIS.md: "The self-correction move appears constantly. The character states something, then refines or undercuts it. It makes the voice feel like a mind in motion rather than a composed narrator." Also AGENTS.md Sentence Rhythm Lock: "Use pivots and self-corrections naturally. The style likes a statement, then a correction, then a sharper restatement."

The passage has strong logic but zero self-correction, zero "or rather," zero "scratch that," zero moment of revision. It lands each claim once, cleanly, and moves on. For a passage this long, one self-correction is expected.

**Correction:** Add a self-correction after "before he found better ground" — for example: "before he found better ground. If there was better ground. This hillside probably looked the same in all directions." Or undercut the "guilty man" conclusion: "It looked like something a guilty man did. Which was arguably accurate, but not the point."

**FLAG 12 — Emotional state stated rather than processed (Hard Ban adjacency)**

> "The adrenaline was there, the panic, the stress, but he also noticed some strange excitement and rush of accomplishment. After all those years of struggling and barely being in control of his body. Those fights actually made him feel alive."

Source: AGENTS.md — "No emotional monologues detached from behavior and context." Also: "Do not use inner thoughts to substitute for narration that should carry physical or emotional state." Also Protagonist Lock: "He processes emotions through physical sensation, short internal observation, and forward movement rather than lyrical introspection."

"The adrenaline was there, the panic, the stress" is a clinical list of labelled emotions. "Some strange excitement and rush of accomplishment" is abstract emotional naming. "Those fights actually made him feel alive" is a direct emotional thesis statement — the kind AGENTS.md categorizes under Hard Bans as "emotional monologue detached from behavior." The passage is not filtered through observation or behavior; it names the feelings and then editorializes on their meaning.

This is the most significant single violation in the chapter.

**Correction:** Anchor the emotional content in physical sensation and behavioral observation. Remove the explanatory clause entirely. Example: "He was still shaking — not just from effort. He noticed that. The dead weight of the carcass beside him, the blood drying on his hands, and he was not particularly upset about any of it." Let the reader draw the conclusion. Do not name it.

## 8. Anti-AI Tell Lock

**FLAG 13 — Evenly machined paragraph sequence (post-combat to group arrival)**

The block from "That was when the sound reached him" through "He made the decision in about a second, which was all the time available" constitutes five paragraphs of almost identical register: medium-length, composed, declarative, each closing on a clear statement. No paragraph is noticeably rougher or more lopsided than the others. Every unit is clean and well-formed.

Source: AGENTS.md — "Preserve roughness, surprise, bluntness, and strange edges where they fit the character. Real prose can be lopsided. It should not feel evenly machined."

None of these paragraphs are individually wrong, but taken together they have the texture of controlled, well-paced genre prose rather than a specific mind under physical and social pressure. The self-correction flag above (FLAG 11) is part of this problem — the analytical voice is too composed when it should show roughness.

**Correction:** Introduce at least one lopsided beat — an observation that runs long because Adrian can't stop himself from cataloguing, or a sentence that breaks off and starts again. The self-correction suggested in FLAG 11 would address this.

**FLAG 14 — Detail that reads as generically evocative rather than character-filtered**

> "The light had gone golden and flat across the hillside and the mountains were darker in the distance than they had been an hour ago."

Source: AGENTS.md Anti-AI Tell Lock: "Details must arrive because the current POV character would notice them, care about them, compare them, or judge them. Flag any detail that feels generically evocative rather than specific to this character's priorities."

Adrian is an infrastructure surveyor. He reads functional surfaces. A generic "golden light / darker mountains" observation has no character filter — it is atmospheric scene-setting that could belong to any third-person fantasy chapter. Adrian would notice the light because it tells him something: time remaining, visibility, temperature drop. The detail as written carries only atmosphere.

**Correction:** Filter the observation through function: "The light had gone golden and flat, which meant he had two hours at most before the temperature dropped enough to matter." Or cut it entirely and let the waystation reference carry the forward pressure.

**FLAG 15 — Paragraph that reads as neutral narrator rather than filtered mind**

> "He did not know this man. He did not know this land, this group, or what waited below. He had one night's water left, his ribs were sending a throbbing pain with every breath, and the only leverage he had was a dead pig."

The opening two sentences are direct negation statements with no analytical texture — no "which was fine, he'd worked from less" or "none of which was information yet." They state Adrian's ignorance as a neutral narrator would tabulate it. The third sentence similarly reads as an inventory delivered by an omniscient hand, though it contains good physical detail (ribs, water).

Source: AGENTS.md — "Free indirect style is mandatory. Even neutral-looking narration should still feel colored by his priorities and phrasing."

**Correction:** Introduce Adrian's analytical voice into the inventory: "He did not know this man, this land, or what the group's tolerance for loose ends looked like — which left him with one night's water, ribs that were making their case loudly, and a dead pig as the only asset he could identify."

**FLAG 16 — Closing paragraph reads as composed reflection, not forward push**

> "But the man walking beside him had come down from a higher ridge, spoken a for everyone else unknown language without explanation, and handled six armed people and a volatile situation with the ease of someone who had done this particular thing many times across many places. And he had done it without hurry, without threat, and without, as far as Adrian could tell, any genuine concern for how it resolved."

Source: PROSE_ANALYSIS.md — "The closing line should feel like the last thing this person would think before moving on — not a thesis sentence for the chapter." Also AGENTS.md: "End on momentum, not on idle reflection."

The penultimate paragraph is a composed analytical summary of Keiran's qualities, delivered in two long parallel sentences. It reads as an authorial thesis on what Keiran represents. It does not push forward — it reflects back. The actual final sentence ("He adjusted his grip on the spear and kept walking") is strong and forward-pushing, but it follows a summary paragraph that undercuts it by explaining what Adrian has concluded.

**Correction:** Cut or radically compress the Keiran summary paragraph. Let "He adjusted his grip on the spear and kept walking" follow a shorter observation — something Adrian notices in the moment (Keiran's pace, the angle of the descent, something about the group ahead) — rather than a retrospective analysis. The forward momentum of the final line is correct; the paragraph before it needs to earn the cut to it.

## 9. Hard Bans

**FLAG 17 — Hollow action line**

> "Adrian stared at him for a moment."

Source: AGENTS.md Final Self-Check — "Are there hollow action lines — sentences that report what Adrian did without any sensory texture, micro-judgment, or POV filter? ('He watched them.' / 'Adrian stared at him for a moment.' / 'He took it.') — these are stage directions, not prose."

This exact construction — "Adrian stared at him for a moment" — is given as the canonical example of a hollow action line in AGENTS.md. It appears twice in the chapter.

First instance (after "You speak English"):
> "Adrian stared at him for a moment."

Second instance (after the observation about watching):
> "Adrian stared at him for a moment. 'You watched.'"

Both are stage directions. Neither puts Adrian in his body. The first follows a sentence where English has just been spoken to him in a world where he had no reason to expect it; this is a significant cognitive shock that deserves more than a stage direction. The second is Adrian processing the revelation that Keiran watched the whole fight.

**Correction, instance 1:** Replace with a filtered reaction: "Something in his chest went flat for a second. English. Actual English." Or: "He did not say anything immediately, because his brain was still catching up to the word."

**Correction, instance 2:** Replace with: "He held that for a second. Keiran had been up there the whole time." Then let "You watched" follow as dialogue without the hollow setup.

**FLAG 18 — Hollow action line (second type)**

> "He did not move."

Appears in the standoff sequence. On its own, this is borderline — it is short, but it could function as a deliberate beat. However, it follows "Two of them adjusted their positions in response, spreading wider along the slope in a way that was not aggressive exactly, but not encouraging either" and immediately precedes "*Flanking. They are actually flanking me over a dead pig.*" The flanking thought is in italics and functions as the real reaction. "He did not move" is a stage direction linking the two, not a filtered experience.

Source: AGENTS.md — same Final Self-Check rule.

**Correction:** Either cut it (let the thought follow directly from the action description) or replace with a physical anchor: "Adrian kept his feet where they were, which was all he had."

**FLAG 19 — Hollow action line**

> "Adrian looked at the group, then back at him."

Source: AGENTS.md — same rule. This is a pure stage direction — camera blocking described as prose. Adrian's analytical habit means any time he looks at something, he is reading it. What does he see when he looks at the group at this moment? What does he see when he looks back at Keiran?

**Correction:** Replace with what the looking actually yields: "He glanced at the group — still watching, still keeping their distance — and then back at Keiran." Or embed the observation inside a longer sentence that carries the character's filter.

**FLAG 20 — Hollow action line**

> "He bent and picked up the broken knife handle from the grass, turned it once in his fingers, and put it back in his pocket. The broken end was useless but the act of leaving it behind felt wrong."

The first sentence is stage direction. The second provides the justification for it — "the act of leaving it behind felt wrong" — which is good character texture. But the two sentences together are doing inefficient work: the stage direction followed by the emotional explanation rather than an integrated filtered moment.

Source: AGENTS.md — the hollow action line rule applies to "Adrian took it" constructions. This is an extended version of that.

**Correction:** Integrate the feeling into the action: "He picked up the broken knife handle, turned it once — the broken end was useless and the weight wrong — and put it back in his pocket. Leaving it would have felt like giving up on the question of whether it had been a good knife."

**FLAG 21 — Emotional monologue detached from behavior (Hard Ban)**

Already flagged under FLAG 12 above. Quoted again for completeness:

> "The adrenaline was there, the panic, the stress, but he also noticed some strange excitement and rush of accomplishment. After all those years of struggling and barely being in control of his body. Those fights actually made him feel alive."

Source: AGENTS.md Hard Bans — "No emotional monologues detached from behavior and context." This passage names feelings clinically, then editorializes on their meaning with no grounding in present physical sensation or behavioral action.

**FLAG 22 — Typo that breaks the POV filter (secondary)**

> "I had to fight a scaled boar. Of cource I am injured."

"Of cource" is a typo for "of course." This is a line-level error, not a style-rule violation per the auditor's scope, but it is noted here because the dialogue is otherwise strong and the error disrupts the reading of a key banter line.

Also within the same exchange: "Yes," Keiran said, in the tone of someone confirming a detail they found mildly interesting. 'That was the part I was paricularly enjoying.'" — "paricularly" is a typo for "particularly."

These are notation items, not flags against AGENTS.md or PROSE_ANALYSIS.md rules.

## Overall Compliance Score: 5 / 10

**Dominant failure pattern:** Three of the chapter's formatted inner thought instances violate the no-inner-thought-during-combat rule — the most explicit and non-negotiable prohibition in AGENTS.md. Every one of these thoughts (*No. No, I am not dying to a mutant pig on a hillside* / *If it gets those legs under it properly...* / *Just die. Please just die*) appears during direct physical combat, where the rule requires narration and physical description to carry the scene entirely. Alongside this, the chapter contains a hard-ban violation (emotional monologue in the post-combat beat that names feelings and editorializes on their meaning), four hollow action lines including one instance of the exact construction AGENTS.md names as its canonical example, and a closing paragraph sequence that summarizes rather than pushes forward. The chapter's dialogue mechanics and analytical voice are largely functional, and the Keiran introduction sequence carries genuine voice distinction. The violations are concentrated in the combat section and the immediate post-combat emotional processing paragraph, both of which require structural revision before line-level work is useful.
