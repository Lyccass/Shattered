## Structural Tells

### Rule of Three — two hits

**Hit 1:**

> "Cable trunks disappeared into the surrounding walls in grouped runs instead of neat hidden feeds. Inspection hatches sat at regular intervals around the inner frame, plain and practical. Somebody had expected known failures and built access around them."

Three consecutive sentences of near-identical construction — observation, observation, conclusion — applied to the arch's engineering. The third sentence is deductive closure on the first two. A human writer would fold the conclusion into one of the observations rather than delivering it as a separate beat.

**Fix:** "Cable trunks ran into the walls in grouped runs rather than neat hidden feeds, and the inspection hatches spaced around the frame said somebody had expected known failures and built access around them."

**Hit 2:**

> "Cold air pressed out across his hands, carrying oil, dust, and the stale mineral stink of closed places that had still been opened often enough to stay dangerous."

"Oil, dust, and the stale mineral stink" is a three-item sensory list. The third item is the one that carries information ("opened often enough to stay dangerous"); the first two are atmospheric padding added for structural completeness.

**Fix:** "Cold air pressed out across his hands, carrying the stale mineral stink of closed places that had still been opened often enough to stay dangerous."

---

### Negative Parallelism — two hits

**Hit 1:**

> "Not recently, but later than Helix, which did not help much."

The negative pivot ("not recently, but") is a mechanical X / not-X construction. The parenthetical self-qualification ("which did not help much") reads as an authorial gloss on Adrian's reasoning rather than the reasoning itself.

**Fix:** "Later than Helix. Not recent enough to matter."

**Hit 2:**

> "It was not Helix-old but later than that, later enough for the coat, later enough for the ration wrappers, and later enough that this bay had been used by somebody who knew what it was for."

"Later enough for... later enough for... and later enough that" is a triple anaphora built on a negative baseline. The triple repetition is structural ornamentation — the third clause does all the work; the first two are completeness filler.

**Fix:** "Not Helix-old — later, by the coat and the ration wrappers, by someone who had known exactly what this bay was for."

---

### Uniform Sentence Length — one hit

> "Four legs and no visible eyes. The head narrowed to a wedge of bone and cartilage with a mouth set too far back, opening sideways more than down. It paused half clear of the wall and worked its jaw at the air."

The three sentences measure approximately 9, 22, and 16 words. They share an identical rhythm: short declarative / long declarative / medium declarative. The word-count regularity in the creature description reads as generated padding between beats.

**Fix:** Collapse the first two sentences — "The head narrowed to a wedge of bone and cartilage, four legs under a hairless frame, no visible eyes, the mouth set too far back and hinged sideways."

---

### Thesis-Antithesis-Synthesis Reasoning — one hit

> "So not sight, then. Vibration, smell, or something worse."

The logic is too tidy. The three alternatives are listed in ascending severity, which is an organised rhetorical move rather than a mind moving under stress. A human writer under combat conditions would arrive at one guess, not a ranked list.

**Fix:** "Vibration, then, or smell. Or something worse that didn't have a name yet."

### Parenthetical Explanations Mid-Sentence — two hits

**Hit 1:**

> "The tool still hanging there was not a weapon unless the person holding it had run out of better options, which, granted, was how most weapons started."

The clause "which, granted, was how most weapons started" is an authorial observation dressed as Adrian's wit. It explains the irony of the preceding sentence for the reader. If the parenthetical stays, cut "granted" — it is a hedging tic that softens what should be a flat observation.

**Hit 2:**

> "It took longer than he wanted, long enough for both shoulders to start shaking under the strain."

"Long enough for both shoulders to start shaking under the strain" restates "longer than he wanted" with a physical clarification. Both clauses together is double-handling.

**Fix:** "His shoulders were shaking before the thing finally stopped."

---

### Over-Symmetrical Paragraph Endings — one hit

The fight sequence contains three creature kills, each ending on a system-or-body pulse beat:

- First kill: *"A hard pulse struck under his sternum and spread inward, hot and wrong..."*
- Second kill: *"The second pulse hit under his sternum as the body went slack, smaller than the first and deeper..."*
- Third kill: *"The third pulse did not strike. It drew inward instead..."*

The escalating variation is deliberate, but the three-beat structure is mechanically mirrored — each kill closes on a Runeheart response in the same positional slot. One of the three should be denied its closing beat entirely, forcing the reader to feel the asymmetry. The second kill is the weakest candidate — cut that beat and let the absence make the third land harder.

## Narrative Red Flags

### Telling Emotions with Clinical Precision — two hits

**Hit 1:**

> "Useful to know. Late, but useful."

Too composed for a man bleeding through a borrowed coat in a room with two dead creatures. The emotion being managed is embarrassment at discovering the information too late, but it is filed away instead of felt.

**Fix:** Drop "Late, but useful" — "Useful to know" alone is sufficient, and the lateness is obvious from context.

**Hit 2:**

> "Adrian stood there with a dead creature pinned to the end of an inferior maintenance pipe and did not move for a moment because movement seemed, frankly, a bit ambitious."

"Frankly" is an authorial insertion that labels the ironic distance instead of enacting it. A human writer at this register would trust the understatement.

**Fix:** "...did not move for a moment because movement seemed, at this point, a bit ambitious."

---

### Protagonist Growth Signalled by Explicit Statement — one hit

> "Hurt was still hurt here."

This is a stated conclusion that translates the demonstrated fact into a statement. The observation that hurting the creature changes its behaviour is already present in the action — "The first one, the one he had punched through the chest earlier, was moving more carefully now. Dark fluid ran down one foreleg." Cut it. The behaviour is the proof.

---

### Every Scene Resolves on a Moral or Emotional Lesson — one hit

> "The third pulse did not strike. It drew inward instead. Heat moved through the centre of him and sank into the Runeheart with the slow certainty of something being claimed rather than merely received."

The phrasing "something being claimed rather than merely received" converts a mechanical event into a thematic declaration about agency and ownership. The chapter would be more unsettling if the final pulse were just heat with no interpretive frame.

**Fix:** Cut "with the slow certainty of something being claimed rather than merely received" — end at "sank into the Runeheart."

---

## Naturalness Score: 8/10

The chapter is the strongest of the three on vocabulary and surface texture — there are no flagged terms, the creature fight is rendered with genuine physical specificity, and the pacing holds. What lowers the score is structural: the three-kill Runeheart escalation is mechanically mirrored in a way that reads as generated pattern, the rule-of-three recurs in sensory and observational passages without necessity, and two of the fight's most effective dry-wit moments are softened by authorial hedges ("granted," "frankly") that do not belong in a character this controlled.
